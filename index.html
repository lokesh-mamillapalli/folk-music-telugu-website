జానపదం Janapada · of the people

Janapada Sahityam

The Telugu that lived in the villages

For centuries, the formal history of the Telugu lands was written by kings, scholars and poets in Granthika Bhasha, a strict, Sanskrit-influenced form of literary Telugu.

But much of Telugu culture was also shaped and preserved by ordinary people in villages, forests and fields. Farmers, shepherds, mothers and travelling performers passed down their stories, experiences and traditions through Janapada Sahityam—folk songs that used native Dravidian words, local rhythms and the language people actually spoke in their everyday lives.

గ్రాంథిక భాష

Granthika Bhasha

Classical Telugu, codified from the 11th century. Heavy with Sanskrit, and bound by strict grammar.

Two regions, two voices

One language, shaped by different lands

Both traditions share Telugu, but geography, political history and daily work gave them different sounds, themes and rhythms. That is why this archive keeps them apart.

ఆంధ్రప్రదేశ్

Andhra Pradesh

Coastal Andhra · Rayalaseema

Shaped by the Godavari and Krishna deltas and the Bay of Bengal. Fishermen sing as they haul their nets, farmers sing of rain and harvest, and the songs are full of village romance, lullabies and temple fairs.

  • SoundMelodic and flowing, with soft consonants and stretched vowels
  • ThemesLove and teasing, devotion, marriage, farming, lullabies
  • LanguageOlder Dravidian vocabulary, with few Urdu or Persian words
  • PerformanceBurrakatha, Harikatha, Thappeta Gullu

తెలంగాణ

Telangana

The Deccan plateau

Shaped by dry, rain-dependent farmland, forests and a long history of feudal struggle. The songs include Bathukamma and Bonalu, the legend of Sammakka and Sarakka, and the voice of peasants against the Dora–Vetti system.

  • SoundPercussive and powerful, driven by Dappu and Teenmaar beats
  • ThemesResistance, labour, migration, drought, regional pride
  • LanguageInfluenced by Dakhni Urdu, Persian and Marathi
  • PerformanceOggu Katha, Dappu dance, Bathukamma songs

How folk Telugu sings

Language that bends to rhythm

Our linguistic analysis found that singers reshape words for breath, beat and feeling. These are some of the patterns we documented.

Andhra Pradesh

Deaffrication

The classical “ch” softens to “s”, which is easier to sustain through long melodies and hours of singing.

Chinnadhanni→Sinnadhanni

Andhra Pradesh

Apheresis

The first sound of a word is dropped so the line moves faster, as in the title of Bandenaka Bandi.

Bandi venaka bandi→Bandenaka bandi

Andhra Pradesh

Rhetorical endings

Suffixes like -vayo, -ro, -amma, -ayya add longing or warmth without changing the dictionary meaning.

“Neeveda konikitivayo”

Telangana

Clipped verb endings

Standard endings give way to sharp, closed forms that match drum-based singing.

Poyinadu→PoyinduKottinadu→Kottindu

Telangana

Layered suffixes

Location endings are stacked for rhythmic bounce and emotional weight. Gundellona means “inside the heart”.

-lo→-lona-llalla

Telangana

Deaspiration

Breath-heavy, Sanskrit-influenced sounds are softened for loud outdoor performance.

Bhakti→BaktiBhayam→Bayam

Words from the village

A vocabulary classical Telugu left out

Classical literature leaned on words borrowed from Sanskrit. Folk songs use the words of everyday village life instead: native Dravidian words for tools, crops, food and work and, in Telangana, words absorbed from Dakhni Urdu, Persian and Marathi. These are the words documented in our analysis.

మంచె

Manche

A raised wooden watchtower in the fields, the practical centre of crop-guarding and rural romance.

గుణపాము

Gunapamu

A heavy iron crowbar used for digging. In Ambaraye, the “-mu” ending is added to fit the rhythm.

కవ్వాము

Kavvamu

A traditional wooden churning staff, used by women churning buttermilk.

దబ్బనాలో

Dabbanaalo

A large, thick iron needle or awl, used for heavy work like stitching gunny bags.

పగ్గాలు

Paggaalu

బండ్లోళ్ళు

బొర్ర

పిందె

చద్ది కూడు

Rhythm & melody

Music made for everyone to join

Folk music values energy, emotion and participation over technical perfection. The tunes are simple and repetitive so a whole village can sing along, even on first hearing.

Melody

Raga-like frameworks

Singers never trained in classical music, yet over generations their melodies came to loosely resemble light Carnatic ragas. These simple, expressive scales carry well in open spaces.

MohanamKharaharapriyaKamasMadhyamavati

Rhythm

Misra Chapu, seven beats

A common seven-beat cycle whose uneven, swinging pulse suits storytelling and devotional songs. Villagers keep the rhythm with claps, feet and voices rather than formal counting.

Form

Call and response

A lead singer sings a line and the crowd answers with a chorus. It is easy to join, and a performance can go on through a night of celebration.

About the project

Documenting an oral heritage before it fades

Many of these songs survive only through listening and performance, and they are slowly disappearing as village life changes. This archive is part of a research project with five aims.

  1. Documentation

    Collect devotional, festival, work, love, tribal, protest and social songs, along with when, how and by whom they are sung.

  2. Linguistic study

    Study regional dialects, pronunciation, sentence structure and local vocabulary.

  3. Musical & cultural study

    Examine rhythms, native instruments and the social purpose each song serves.

  4. Digital preservation

    Publish Telugu lyrics, transliterations, translations, audio and cultural notes.

  5. Regional comparison

    Keep Andhra Pradesh and Telangana distinct, and compare how each shaped its music.
