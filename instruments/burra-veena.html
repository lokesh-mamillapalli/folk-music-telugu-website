బుర్ర వీణ

Burra Veena

Also known as Burraveena (బుర్రవీణ)

A three-stringed instrument made from a dried gourd and a bamboo stick and played with little sticks hung with bells, used to accompany tatvalu and devotional songs. Its last known player, Dasari Kondappa of Damaragidda in Narayanpet district, received the Padma Shri in 2024.

About

The Burra Veena (బుర్ర వీణ) is a stringed instrument described as unique to Telangana, made from bamboo, a gourd shell and metal strings1,5. It has been called an amalgamation of many stringed instruments and is said to be closely related to the Bhoomi Veena and to the tribal villadi vadyalu, instruments played with a bow string1,2. A Telugu account places it in the same family as the ektara and the tambura3.

Only one player is known today: Dasari Kondappa of Damaragidda village in Narayanpet district, a third-generation Burra Veena player who was awarded the Padma Shri in 2024 as its guardian5,4. A 2021 review of the study Mula Dhwani likewise noted that only one person alive could play the Burra Veena7.

How it is made

Kondappa makes his own instruments, as his forefathers did, and says one Burra Veena takes about a year to make1,2,4. First a mature bottle gourd or pumpkin is chosen, which can take eight months to a year to ripen on the vine, and it is then dried for about two months. The gourd must not crack, because a cracked gourd will not sound3.

When the gourd has dried its seeds are removed and a straight bamboo stick is fitted into it. A wooden bridge is fixed on the gourd with wax, three iron strings are tied along the stick, and pegs made of mango wood are fitted to the far end of the bamboo so that the strings give different notes3,1.

The sticks used to sound the strings are made from slivers of bamboo: small bells are tied with wire in a ring onto one bamboo stick, and another stick is fixed to that knot of bells3. Kondappa decorates the gourd with white vibhuti and kumkum namam marks3. He has said that suitable gourds (he names the ash gourd) have become hard to find, so new instruments are difficult to make4.

How it is played

Unlike other veenas, which are played with the fingers, the Burra Veena has to be played with the help of sticks4. Kondappa plays it with small tingling bells and treats it like a percussion instrument: his right hand provides the bass and percussion, and his left hand the melody1,2.

He says he can produce 24 different sounds on it, apart from the ragas, and that people who already play the dakki drum, cymbals or dappu find it easier to learn2,4. He performs seated on a mat on the floor, and before beginning a song he bows to the instrument with folded hands3.

Beliefs & practices

Kondappa belongs to the Mala Dasari community, a Dalit community described as dependents (ashrita) of the Malas. Mala Dasaris spread Vaishnava devotion, and Kondappa travels with his decorated instrument to the homes of the community he serves1,3.

The family once made its living through house-to-house rounds, singing with the Burra Veena and collecting grain as alms in an akshaya patra. Kondappa says he gave up these rounds, hurt by people's remarks that he was able-bodied and could work for a living, and began giving stage performances instead4.

The art is handed down within the family. Kondappa learned to play from his father Venkanna and learned the songs from his elder brother Kondanna; he began playing at sixteen and is now teaching his grandson Bhanuprasad4. He has said that whenever he plays he feels a deep connection to his ancestors and a responsibility to keep the art alive for future generations6. Professor Manoja of Palamuru University has suggested that caste prejudice is one reason few people have come forward to learn it1.

His performances end with the name of Govinda, remembering his favourite deity, Sri Venkateswara3.

Where you will hear it

The Burra Veena accompanies tatvalu, socio-religious and philosophical songs, which Kondappa sings in Telugu and Kannada as well as Hindi5,3. His repertoire also includes songs from the Ramayana, Mahabharata and Bhagavad Gita, Satya Harishchandra, Bathukamma songs, the Kalagnana tatvalu of Veerabrahmendra Swamy, kirtanas of Manyamkonda Hanumaddasu, Chenchu kommalu and Dharmanga charitra2,3,4.

A team led by Professor Jayadhir Tirumala Rao introduced Kondappa to a wider public in 2016. The instrument was shown at the Adya Kala exhibition at the State Art Gallery in Madapur in 2021, and Kondappa has performed in an Indo-French music collaboration in Hyderabad and sung in the background music of the Telugu film Balagam3.

The instrument is critically endangered. In 2019 The News Minute described Kondappa as the only person in Telangana who could play it; none of his children took up the art, though he says he is ready to teach anyone who wants to learn1,4.
