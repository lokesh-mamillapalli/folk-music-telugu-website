Chirutalu

Also known as Chiratalu (చిరతలు), Chidatalu (చిడతలు), Chitikelu (చిటికెలు), Gidigillu (older literary word) (గిడిగిళ్లు), Castanets / wooden clappers

Chirutalu are a pair of wooden clappers, often fitted with jingles or finger rings, that a singer clicks in one hand to keep time. Harikatha storytellers and Sankranti Haridasus carry small ones, while village bhajana troupes and Telangana's Chirutala Ramayanam use larger, carved pairs.

About

Chirutalu (also written chiratalu or chidatalu) are hand-held wooden clappers used to keep rhythm in Telugu devotional and narrative performance. Telugu Wikipedia describes them as two small pieces of wood with small bells (gajjelu) attached and rings for the fingers, used mainly by Harikatha performers and bhajana singers1. C. P. Brown's Telugu-English Dictionary (1903) explains chirata or chiruta as a piece of wood and also 'a castanet'2, lists chitikelu as 'castanets'3, and gives the older word gidigillu as 'castanets, or a kind of rattle', equating it with chiratalu and chitikelu4.

The instrument comes in different sizes. Harikatha narrators use small chirutalu, while a similar but larger instrument is used in group forms such as Chekka Bhajana1. Some Telangana art forms are named after the clappers, such as Chirutala Ramayanam and Chirutala Bhajana5,6. Wooden clappers with jingles of this general type are found elsewhere in India under names such as kartal or khartal, and in Maharashtra as chiplya7.

How it is made

The basic instrument is a pair of wooden pieces. According to Telugu Wikipedia, small bells (gajjelu) are fixed in the middle of the pieces and each piece has a ring through which the player's fingers pass; when the pair is played, the jingle of the bells mixes pleasantly with the sound of the wood1.

Larger clappers used by dancing troupes are carved and fitted with metal discs. Describing the clappers of Chekka Bhajana, which the same author also calls chirutalu, Mikkilineni Radhakrishna Murthy records pieces of wood about a foot long, decorated with carving, with two round iron or brass discs fixed on each side so that they ring when struck8.

How it is played

Chirutalu are held and clicked in one hand, leaving the performer free to sing, dance or play another instrument. The Harikatha narrator holds chirutalu in his hand and wears ankle bells, singing, narrating, acting and dancing for about three hours, usually accompanied by a violin, veena or harmonium and a mridangam9,10.

The Sankranti Haridasu strums a tambura on his shoulder and keeps time with chirutalu in the other hand, singing and dancing with bells tied to his ankles as he stops before each house11. English-language reports describe the same practice as carrying 'chidathalu' in one hand and a tambura in the other12,13.

In group forms the whole troupe plays together. In Chirutala Ramayanam, thirty to forty young village labourers plant a pole with flags in an open space outside the village, hold chirutalu, tie bells to their ankles and sing and dance; the author compares it to Kolatam, except that the troupe dances with chirutalu and its members act the roles of Ramayana and Mahabharata stories5. In Pandari bhajana the dancers hold chirutalu while singing and miming the songs, accompanied by dholak or maddela, kanjira, tambura, talalu and harmonium14.

Beliefs & practices

Chirutalu belong mainly to devotional performance. Mikkilineni describes elderly villagers sitting to sing Hari bhajanas and Pandari bhajanas with hand cymbals and chirutalu, and devotees singing Jayadeva's ashtapadis, Purandaradasa's and Annamacharya's songs with chirutalu, clappers, cymbals, tamburas and ankle bells15.

The Haridasu's round with tambura and chirutalu is a Dhanurmasam and Sankranti custom. He bathes at dawn, wears saffron cloth, namam marks and garlands, and carries an akshaya patra on his head; when a household offers rice he kneels to receive it and blesses the family11. A 2018 Yo! Vizag feature adds that he sings 'Harilo Ranga Hari' and Ramadasu's kirtanas, is said never to stop to ask for alms or look back, and that villagers wash his feet and seek his blessing12.

Group troupes learn from a guru. Chirutala Ramayanam troupes engage a guru to teach them during the summer5, and before a Chekka Bhajana performance the troupe worships pictures of the gods, breaks a coconut, and holds their chirutalu while prostrating before they begin8.

Where you will hear it

Harikatha, the solo musical storytelling form that Adibhatla Narayana Dasu shaped into a distinctive art, is performed at night, especially during Ganapati Navaratri, Dasara, Krishna Jayanti, Mukkoti Ekadasi and Sankranti; long Ramayana, Mahabharata or Bhagavata series can run for fifteen days to a month9.

Haridasus singing from house to house with chirutalu appear in Telugu villages and towns during Dhanurmasam, the month leading up to Sankranti, and are otherwise rarely seen during the year11. A 2020 BBC News Telugu report described the Haridasu as a tradition that is disappearing in modern times13.

Chirutala Ramayanam is widespread in Telangana and described as a very old form in Karimnagar district. Mikkilineni (1992) notes that its popularity fell after cinema arrived but that troupes were still active in villages such as Thotapalli, Cherla Buthkur, Chintakunta and Veenavanka5. Recent Telugu television news reports show Chirutala Ramayanam performances in Karimnagar district in 2026 and an eight-hour non-stop Chirutala Bhajana performance there in 2025 aimed at a record16,6.

Chirutalu are also used in Pandari bhajana, a dance-centred bhajana that came from Maharashtra and in which children take part in large numbers14, and a Chirutala Bhajana troupe took part in the cultural procession held in Hyderabad on 17 September 2022 for the Telangana Jateeya Samaikyatha Vajrotsavalu celebrations1.

Research notes: points where sources differ or could not be confirmed
  • English-language sources often call these clappers 'cymbals' (for example Yo! Vizag's 'chidathalu (cymbals)' and English Wikipedia's Harikatha article, which says the storyteller uses 'a pair of cymbals'), whereas Telugu Wikipedia and Brown's dictionary describe wooden clappers or castanets. Some narrators may use small cymbals instead; this could not be settled from the sources.
  • Spelling varies: చిరుతలు (Telugu Wikipedia, Mikkilineni), చిరతలు (Mikkilineni's Harikatha chapter, S. Gangappa) and చిడతలు (BBC News Telugu). In everyday Telugu చిటిక/చిటికెలు also means a finger snap, so 'chitikelu' is ambiguous; Brown (1903) is the only source found that uses it for castanets.
  • The word chirutalu is used loosely: Mikkilineni also calls Kolatam sticks 'kolatapu chirutalu' and the Chekka Bhajana clappers 'chirutalu'. The large carved clappers of Telangana's Chirutala Bhajana appear to be the same kind of instrument as the chekkalu of Chekka Bhajana, which are described in a separate file (chekkalu.json).
  • No source was found on who makes chirutalu, what woods are used for the small Harikatha type, or making rituals specific to the instrument.
  • The list of active Chirutala Ramayanam villages comes from a 1992 book and may be out of date. The 2025 and 2026 news reports were known only from their titles and short descriptions.
