Dappu

Also known as Tappeta (dappu drumming is also called tappetla vayidyam) (తప్పెట), Kanaka tappetlu (Rayalaseema) (కనక తప్పెట్లు), Palaka (Andhra Pradesh name for the wooden frame; in some areas for the drum itself) (పలక), Gundu (Telangana name for the wooden frame) (గుండు)

A large, flat, one-sided frame drum beaten with two sticks, made and played for generations mainly by the Madiga community. It sounds at weddings, goddess festivals, processions, village announcements and funerals, and has become a symbol of Dalit pride and protest.

About

The dappu (డప్పు) is a flat, round frame drum with a skin stretched over one side, beaten with two sticks; C. P. Brown's Telugu-English Dictionary (1903) simply calls it 'a large drum' and a drummer a dappuvadu.11,1,7 It is closely tied to the Madiga community of Telangana and Andhra Pradesh, who have historically both made and played it, and Firstpost notes that every celebration in a Madiga household begins with the family playing the drum and dancing.1,2

Its beat is part of almost every public occasion in the Telugu countryside: weddings, festivals of village goddesses, jataras, processions and funerals. For generations a drummer walking through the village has also been the way to announce news, from the sale of goods to a panchayat meeting, a practice called chatimpu or dandora.1,3,5,7

The drum has several regional names. Mikkilineni Radhakrishna Murthy's survey of Telugu folk arts says dappu drumming is also called tappetla vayidyam, and that in Rayalaseema the drums are called kanaka tappetlu.5 Firstpost reports that the circular wooden frame is called gundu in Telangana and palaka in Andhra Pradesh, and Telugu Wikipedia adds that in some areas the whole drum is called palaka.1,8 Similar frame drums are known as thappu or parai in Tamil Nadu.1

A village saying quoted by Madiga elders sums up its place in life: 'Penli ayina chavu ayina, dappu debba padilsinde' (పెళ్లైనా, చావైనా డప్పుదెబ్బ పడాల్సిందే) - whether it is a wedding or a death, the dappu must be struck.1,8

How it is made

The frame is a ring of wood, usually neem or tamarind; Mikkilineni names neem and chandra wood.1,2,3,5 The head is animal hide. Sources mention calf skin, the skin of a young male buffalo or goat, and more generally the hides of cattle, buffalo or goats.5,7,1,3

Making a dappu is slow, careful work. The hide is tanned, treated with ash and salt so that it does not rot, and dried. It is then pasted onto the wooden ring with a thin, sticky paste made from tamarind seeds and the bark of tangedu (Tanner's cassia), tied tight with string and left under a weight.2,1 An elderly cobbler interviewed by Firstpost explained that thicker skins went into sandal soles and thinner ones into dappus.1

Fire is part of both making and playing the drum. Once the skin is fixed, the dappu is exposed to fire so that loose edges pull tight.1,2 Drummers warm the head over a flame before playing so that it gives a bright ringing sound, which Mikkilineni describes as 'kanakana'; The Hindu describes drummers at a night performance warming their dappus in the glow of a bonfire, the skin going taut in the heat.5,4,10

Firstpost reports that dappus were sold only in small shops owned by Madigas, because mainstream music shops that sell other leather instruments would not stock them. As Madigas lost access to hides, drums with synthetic or plastic heads became common, especially in towns.1,2

How it is played

The drummer carries the dappu slung from the shoulder and beats it with two sticks.3 The short, thick stick in the right hand, called sirra or sirre, strikes the lower part of the head and creates the rhythm; the long, thin stick in the left hand, called sittikenna pulla (or chitikena pulla), plays near the upper side and controls the pace.1,7 Skilled drummers change the sound further by resting the left palm on the upper rim.7 The News Minute calls the pair of sticks 'chirra-chitike'.2

Each occasion has its own drumming style (debba or daruvu): the beat for a wedding procession is different from the beat for a festival of the gods, and the beat played when someone dies is different again.5,7 A 2021 training course in Hyderabad taught learners at least ten popular jatis (beats) and the human pyramids that dappu troupes build while playing.3

Dappu dance is done by troupes of about ten to twenty drummers wearing ankle bells, often at the head of a procession. It opens with a slow circular invocation called pradhana dappu and uses named steps (adugu) such as ata dappu, okka sira dappu, samidika dappu, madil dappu and gundam dappu.7 In dappula kolatam the dancers hold the drums under the arm and strike each other's sticks as in a stick dance while leaping with belled feet.5

Mikkilineni records virtuoso tricks from weddings in Nizamabad district, where the number of dappus in a wedding procession is a matter of prestige: a drummer plays so that the vibration knocks coins off an upturned dappu on the ground, or picks up currency notes from the ground with his eyelids without losing the rhythm. He also notes that Amrutayya of Kanumuru and Yesu Dasu of Unguturu in Krishna district played mridangam patterns on the dappu and toured with the Praja Natya Mandali.5

Beliefs & practices

Madiga oral tradition tells several stories about how the dappu began. In one, a hunter who accidentally killed a female monkey took home the flat drum her mate had been playing, and his village learnt to play and make it. In another, a man kicked a stone onto a dried hide and its sound frightened away wild animals; folklore also calls the dappu the first instrument early humans made to announce their presence. The oral epics Jambava Puranam, Dakkali Puranam and Baindla Puranam give further accounts of its origin.1,2

By custom the dappu was played only by men, while women joined in the dancing and singing. In recent years some women, among them the singer and activist Vimalakka, have taken up the drum, though this is still not common.1

Because it is made of leather by Madigas, dominant castes treated the dappu as an 'untouchable' instrument, and some Madigas stopped making or playing it out of fear of shame.1,2 Dappu artist and documentary filmmaker Lelle Suresh answers this prejudice with the words: 'When a cow is holy, its skin can't be unholy.'2

From the 1980s the drum became a symbol of Dalit self-respect. After the Karamchedu (1985) and Tsundur (1991) massacres, artists such as Kalekuri Prasad sang about these events to its beat, and the campaign for sub-classification of Scheduled Caste reservations that began in 1994 took the name Madiga Dandora, after the drum-beaten announcement, with 'cheppu-dappu' (sandal and drum) as its emblems of dignity.1,2 Revolutionary cultural troupes such as the Jana Natya Mandali and singers like Gaddar made the dappu central to their performances.7,1

Where you will hear it

The dappu accompanies weddings, festivals of folk goddesses, jataras, car festivals of deities, worship of veerulu (hero deities), Veerabhadra performances and funerals across Telugu villages.3,5,7 During Bonalu in Hyderabad, dappu beats resound in the processions in which Potharaju dances ahead of the goddess's offerings.9

It is the main instrument of dappu dance and dappula kolatam, and Firstpost notes that the Chindu dance evolved alongside it.5,1 In Telangana, Mikkilineni describes the Jogu people, a Dalit sub-group, dancing with dappus at weddings and festivals and also while carrying their dead to the cremation ground.6

The drum has powered mass movements on Telugu soil: Naxalite and Maoist cultural troupes, Dalit movements and the public meetings of the Telangana statehood movement all relied on dappu artists, and political parties of every colour now bring dappu troupes to their rallies.2,3

Artists and scholars say the art has been undervalued. PhD scholar Andey Bhaskar points out that no dappu artist has been nominated for honours such as the Padma Shri, and in March 2021 the Telangana Department of Language and Culture ran a 15-day dappu training programme in Hyderabad for about fifty learners, including four women, to revive the form.2,3
