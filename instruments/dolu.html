Dolu

Also known as Tavil / Thavil (Tamil name for the dolu of the nadaswaram ensemble) (తవిల్), Oggu dolu (Telangana) (ఒగ్గు డోలు), Veerappa dolla (Kurnool and Anantapur) (వీరప్ప డొల్ల), Kommu dolu (Koya) (కొమ్ము డోలు)

A barrel drum with skins on both ends. It is the rhythm partner of the nadaswaram (sannayi) in the auspicious mangala vadyam music of weddings and temples, and it is also the big drum of Telangana's Oggu Dolu dance, the Koya Kommu Koya dance and many rituals and processions.

About

Dolu (డోలు) is a general Telugu word for a drum. Dictionaries define it as a charma vadyam (an instrument sounded on a stretched skin) and trace it to Sanskrit ḍhōla, and old poetry names it alongside other instruments, as in a verse of the Accha Telugu Ramayanam quoted in the Sabdaratnakaram that lists bheri, dolu, tappeta, kale, flutes and tudumu together.1

In the classical mangala vadyam (auspicious music) of South India, the dolu is the drum that accompanies the nadaswaram, which in Andhra is called sannayi. Dolu artist and Telugu scholar Dr. Bhusurapalli Venkateswarlu writes that the same drum is called tavil in the Tamil region, that old musical treatises called it shringabheri or dindima, and that it is said to derive from Shiva's damaru.2

In folk and tribal traditions the name covers other large barrel drums, such as the brass Oggu dolu of the Kuruma and Golla communities of Telangana and the drums worn by Koya men in the Kommu Koya dance, which is also called Kommu dolu. The Telugu Akademi's regional dialect dictionary (2004) also records dolu as the Telangana dialect word for the drum called ḍhaṅkā, which is tudumu in the North Coastal Andhra (Kalinga) dialect.3,4,1

How it is made

Bhusurapalli Venkateswarlu describes the dolu of the nadaswaram ensemble as closed with skin on both sides, one side played with the hand and the other with a stick about a foot long.2

M. A. Rauf's account of tribal instruments, reproduced by Mikkilineni Radhakrishna Murthy, describes the tribal dolu as a hollowed wooden body with deer skin or goat skin on both ends, kept taut with pieces of bamboo and ropes.5

The Oggu dolu of Telangana is about a metre long and drum-shaped. Mikkilineni writes that it is now made of brass but was formerly made of wood, with goat skin fitted on both sides and tightened with ropes, and that it gives a deep, resonant sound. It is beaten with sticks of jilledu (calotropis) or custard-apple wood. Similar drums in Kurnool and Anantapur districts of Rayalaseema are called Veerappa dolla.3

How it is played

A mangala vadyam group (melam) has two nadaswarams, two dolus, a drone (sruti) and cymbals (talam), and a nadaswaram concert opens with the dolu. Bhusurapalli argues that because two dolu players both cooperate and compete in every concert, dolu playing developed far more elaborate counting and rhythmic patterns than other skin drums.2

Rauf describes a second kind of tribal dolu that is hung from the neck with the drum resting against the belly, and struck with a bamboo stick in one hand and the fingers, sometimes the palm, of the other. In the Kommu Koya dance, men wear a large dolu from the neck and play it in rhythm while stepping in a circle in the dance called Permichor.5,4

Oggu dolu players dance in circles while drumming vigorous patterns and perform feats: holding the drum's ropes in their teeth while they dance and play, or sitting with the drum against the back and playing it with their arms turned behind them.3

Beliefs & practices

The mangala vadyam is inseparable from Hindu weddings and temple worship. Bhusurapalli writes that as soon as the turmeric root is broken for a wedding in a Hindu home, the mangala vadyam must sound, and that the 'tom tom tom' with which the dolu opens a nadaswaram performance recalls the Omkara of Shiva's damaru.2

The Kurumas and Gollas regard the Oggu dolu as a mangala vadyam at weddings. Seven or eight dolus are played together at goddess-temple festivals, at jataras and during worship in Shiva temples, and the drum is played at Mallanna festivals and processions and at rites such as going to the water (jaladhi), mailapolu, nagavalli and veerabonam. Before an Oggu Katha the dolu is sounded as a signal, and the whole village gathers at its sound.3

In the Veerabhadra festivals of Veerashaiva devotees, dolus beaten at great speed, together with a special instrument called veerangam, sannayis and conches, drive the ecstatic sword-bearers and the devotees who pierce their tongues with skewers (narasalu). In Veeranatyam, the dance of the Veerakumarulu is spurred on by sannayi, dolu, tambura and tasha.6,7

For the Koyas, the dolu dance is performed at Bhumi Puja, the first-harvest festival, and at life events from birth to death.4

Where you will hear it

As part of the mangala vadyam the dolu is played at weddings, in temples and at festivals throughout Andhra Pradesh and Telangana. Bhusurapalli names first-generation Telugu dolu players such as Penugudurupadu Ankamma, Modukuri Balakotayya, Kuntupalli Guravayya, Parella Ammisetti and Turumella Subbarayudu; later players in the Thanjavur style such as Annavarapu Basavayya and Emani Raghavayya; Tirupati Muniramayya; and Layabrahma Bhusurapalli Adiseshayya, Nidamanuri Lakshminarayana, Vijayawada Kannarao, Munnangi Venkateswarlu and Tarigopula Narayana, noting that their families have lived by the art for generations.2

In folk performance, three players with dolu, cymbals and kanjira stand behind the storytellers of Oggu Katha, and Mikkilineni describes big dolus in the Golla suddulu storytelling and among Telangana's Yadava storytellers. The dolu and a sruti sannayi accompany the Gangireddu (decorated bull) performers, Dommara acrobats beat their dolu to gather the village before a show, and the Mala Jangalu count the dolu among their best-known instruments.3,8,9,10,11

In January 2026, 30 Oggu Dolu artists from Jangaon, Siddipet, Vikarabad, Jagtial, Peddapalli and Sircilla districts, led by Dr. Oggu Ravi, were selected to perform on Kartavya Path in the Republic Day parade, the first time the art form was included.12

Writing in 2006, Bhusurapalli warned that under the din of foreign instruments, nadaswaram and dolu music was surviving 'with its last breath' and the livelihood of young players had become uncertain, and he appealed to the government and cultural bodies to support these musicians.2

Research notes: points where sources differ or could not be confirmed
  • Dolu and dhol: dictionaries derive dolu from Sanskrit ḍhōla (the Andhra-Tamil-Kannada dictionary gives Kannada ḍhōlu), but none of the sources consulted directly compares the Telugu dolu with the North Indian dhol, so no claim is made that they are the same instrument.
  • Dolu and thavil: Bhusurapalli Venkateswarlu states that the dolu of the nadaswaram ensemble is the drum called tavil in Tamil. The folk and tribal drums also called dolu (Oggu dolu, Koya dolu, tribal dolu) are different instruments that share the name.
  • Bhusurapalli's statement that the dolu derives from Shiva's damaru is a traditional view, not a documented history.
  • The Oggu dolu is closely tied to Oggu Katha; if the Oggu page also covers the Oggu dolu, the two pages should be cross-checked. Mikkilineni's statement that it is 'about a metre long' and now made of brass is from 1992 and may not apply to every troupe.
  • Sources often associate nadaswaram and dolu playing with particular hereditary communities, but the sources read for this page (Bhusurapalli) speak only of hereditary families, so no caste is named here.
  • Some sources describe the Koya drum as a large cowhide drum; the material of the Koya dolu was not confirmed in the sources read.
