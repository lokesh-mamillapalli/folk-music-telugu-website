గుమ్మెట

Gummeta

Also known as Gummeta (variant spelling) (గుమ్మెత), Budige (బుడిగె), Budike (బుడికె), Dakki (డక్కీ), Dikki (డిక్కీ), Dimki (name used by the Saradakandru) (డిమ్కీ)

A small pot-shaped drum with a skin over one end and an open mouth at the other. The two side-singers of Burrakatha (Jangam katha) hang it from the shoulder and strike the skin with their fingers while the other hand opens and closes the mouth to change the sound.

About

The gummeta is the drum of the Burrakatha storytelling tradition. Mikkilineni Radhakrishna Murthy writes that the Jangam storytellers are also called Budige Jangalu after the instrument they use in their stories, the budige, which is known in different regions as dakki, dikki or gummeta. Among the art's many names are gummetla katha and dakki katha, and Mikkilineni reports a view that even the name Burrakatha comes from 'burra', said to be another name for the gummeta, alongside the explanation that it refers to the gourd resonator (burra) of the storyteller's tambura.1,2,3

Telugu dictionaries describe the gummeta as a kumbha vadyam (pot drum) and as a small drum. Vavilla's dictionary (1949) quotes Krishnadevaraya's Amuktamalyada, where the gummeta appears in a long list of instruments, and C. P. Brown's dictionary (1903) also cites the word from classical poetry.4,5,6

The word is Dravidian: the Dravidian Etymological Dictionary lists Telugu gummeṭa and gummeta ('a kind of small drum') together with Tamil kummaṭṭam ('a small drum') and Gondi gumela ('kind of drum'). The Purushottama Kaviyamu (1918) instead derives it from Sanskrit gumbhita.7,4

Mikkilineni reports the view of scholars that the veeragumbhita drum, played by a woman while a heroic ballad is sung in the medieval Telugu play Kridabhiramam, is the present-day gummeta, and suggests that the name refers to the drum's deep, muffled (gumbhana) sound.1

How it is made

Accounts differ on the material. Mikkilineni describes the budige of the Jangam storytellers as made of bronze (kanchu) or brass, and notes that budiges are smaller than ordinary gummetas. On one side a cane hoop is fixed and covered with skin; the other side is left uncovered, like the mouth of a water jug (kuja).1,2

The Telugu Wikipedia article describes the gummeta as a clay vessel like a water jug whose bottom is removed and replaced with skin, the mouth end being left open, and English Wikipedia calls the gummetas of Burrakatha earthen drums.8,3

David Courtney describes the gummeta as a South Indian folk drum of which a smaller version is used in Andhra Pradesh for Burrakatha and a slightly larger one in Goa and Karnataka, and places it in the same class of instruments as the tumbak of Kashmir and the Middle Eastern darbuka, while noting that any historical link between them is unclear.9

How it is played

In Burrakatha the main storyteller (kathakudu) plays the tambura and keeps time with metal rings (andelu), while the two side-singers (vantalu) on either side play gummetas hung from the neck or shoulder so that the drum sits under the arm.1,2,3,9

The player strikes the skin with the fingers of the right hand and closes and opens the uncovered mouth with the other hand, producing a deep, muffled tone. Courtney notes that both hands are occasionally used on the skin.2,8,9

Mikkilineni gives the syllables of the side-singers' beat, such as 'daddotimi, tadimi tattaddhimi, takita tatadhimi', with ornamental 'tadiginatom' phrases and cries of 'arare' and 'bhali bhali' to spur the storyteller. He adds that a story can be told with just the three-stroke 'daddotimi', but skilled players use many classical rhythm patterns (varusalu) ending in cadences (muktayimpu), and in scenes of fury the paired gummetas sound like thunderbolts. 'Gummetas are the life of the story and of the storyteller,' he writes.1

Before a night performance, the Budige Jangams play the gummetas loudly at the main spots of the village while going around announcing where the story will be told and inviting everyone to come.2

Beliefs & practices

The gummeta belongs to the Shaiva Jangam storytelling tradition. According to Mikkilineni, Jangams were already singing these stories by about 1150 CE, when Veerashaivism was spreading, and the Budaga (Beda) Jangams also conduct funeral rites for Dalit, Adivasi and other backward-class households, walking ahead of the bier to the cremation ground blowing a conch and ringing a bell.1,2

In older practice whole families performed together and the storyteller's wife sang the vanta. Among the Saradakandru of Telangana, who sing their Sarada katha to a shoulder-held tambura called sarada, a storyteller's two wives sing the vanta, while a storyteller with only one wife is accompanied by her playing the dakki, which they also call dimki.1

Mikkilineni names Burra Pantulu of Khammam, Doddarapu Venkataswamy, Tadikonda Subbayya and Yellamanda as masters of the gummeta; he and Machineni learnt many patterns from Yellamanda and used them to train the Burrakatha troupes of the Praja Natya Mandali.1

Where you will hear it

Burrakatha is performed across Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, and its three-person format of storyteller and two gummeta-playing side-singers carried over from the older Jangam katha into the modern Burrakathas on political and social themes, developed from the 1940s. Christians also used the form to spread their faith: Gera Premayya's Burrakatha 'Pandita Ramabai' includes a verse describing the art, with 'the pair of gummetas sounding tadhimi'.1,3,10

The drum is used in related storytelling traditions. In Rayalaseema the Pichhukuntla, who first used only a bell and a titti, took up the tambura and gummetas under Jangam influence, and Mikkilineni notes that the budiges used by Jangams in Telangana are of the same kind. The Telugu Wikipedia article adds that the gummeta is also used by Oggu Katha tellers and by some women who sing and beg with it alone.11,8

A related clay drum of the Gonds of Adilabad is called gumela. M. A. Rauf, quoted by Mikkilineni, describes it as a hollow cylindrical drum of potter's clay with goat skin stretched over one side, played during the Dandari dance, and a 2021 report lists the 'Ghumela' among the instruments that Gonds and Kolams play before the goddess Jangubai at Dandari-Gussadi.12,13

Mikkilineni lists the districts where Budige Jangams still lived by their traditional occupations in the early 1990s — Srikakulam, Visakhapatnam, East and West Godavari, Krishna, Guntur, Prakasam, Chittoor, Nellore, Nizamabad and Medak — and asked the government to extend to them the facilities given to other scheduled communities.2

Research notes: points where sources differ or could not be confirmed
  • Material: Mikkilineni (1992) describes the Jangams' budige as brass or bronze, while Telugu Wikipedia and English Wikipedia describe the gummeta as earthen (clay). Both kinds may be in use.
  • English Wikipedia calls the gummetas 'earthen drums with two heads', but Mikkilineni, Telugu Wikipedia and David Courtney all describe one skin and an open end. Some present-day troupes may use a two-headed drum in the same role, which could explain the difference; this was not checked in a written source.
  • The name dakki is ambiguous: Brown's and Sankaranarayana's dictionaries define ḍakki as a small hourglass drum (damaru), and the Telangana Padakosam (2010) as a small dappu, whereas Mikkilineni uses dakki as a regional name for the budige or gummeta. It is not certain that the Saradakandru's dakki (dimki) is identical to the Burrakatha gummeta.
  • Sabdaratnakaram and G. N. Reddy's dictionaries give tudumu as a synonym of gummeta; see the Tudumu page.
  • The identification of the veeragumbhita in Kridabhiramam with the gummeta is a scholarly opinion reported by Mikkilineni (citing Tirumala Ramachandra), not a certainty.
  • The use of the gummeta in Oggu Katha is stated only in Telugu Wikipedia and was not confirmed by another source.
