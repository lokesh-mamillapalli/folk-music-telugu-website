Instruments of Telugu Folk Music

The drums, strings, wind instruments, bells and clappers heard in the folk songs of Andhra Pradesh and Telangana. Each page describes how the instrument is made and played, the beliefs and practices around it, and the traditions it accompanies.

Drums అవనద్ధ వాద్యాలు

Drum

Dappu

A large, flat, one-sided frame drum beaten with two sticks, made and played for generations mainly by the Madiga community. It sounds at weddings, goddess festivals, processions, village announcements and funerals, and has become a symbol of Dalit pride and protest.

Drum

Dolu

A barrel drum with skins on both ends. It is the rhythm partner of the nadaswaram (sannayi) in the auspicious mangala vadyam music of weddings and temples, and it is also the big drum of Telangana's Oggu Dolu dance, the Koya Kommu Koya dance and many rituals and processions.

Drum

Gumela

An earthen drum of the Raj Gonds of the Adilabad region, bought from local potters and covered with goat skin. Played in pairs with the palm, it has a soft, mellow tone and accompanies the dance songs of the Dandari festival season before Diwali.

Telangana
Drum

Gummeta

A small pot-shaped drum with a skin over one end and an open mouth at the other. The two side-singers of Burrakatha (Jangam katha) hang it from the shoulder and strike the skin with their fingers while the other hand opens and closes the mouth to change the sound.

Andhra PradeshTelangana
Drum

Jamidika (Jamuku)

A single-headed 'string drum' whose skin is sounded through a gut string that the player pulls and plucks. Called jamidika by the Baindla priest-singers of Telangana and jamuku in north coastal Andhra, it accompanies the stories of Yellamma and other village goddesses.

Drum

Maddela

The Telugu name for a mridangam-type skin drum that accompanies song in folk theatre: Tholu Bommalata shadow puppetry, Chindu Bhagavatam, Yakshaganam and street-theatre troupes, daytime impersonators, kolatam and bhajans, usually together with harmonium and cymbals.

Andhra PradeshTelangana
Drum

Oggu

A small double-headed hourglass drum - the Telangana name for the damaru held by Shiva - which gives the Oggu Katha ballad tradition of the Kuruma and Golla communities its name. It is sounded at the start of the story and in many scenes of the narration.

Telangana
Drum

Pamba

A pair of brass drums beaten with two sticks by the Pambala community, from whom the drum takes its name. It leads village-goddess processions and accompanies the night-long Ankamma stories of the Pambalas; it is used only for worship and ritual, never for concerts.

Drum

Parra

A small double-headed wooden drum of the Raj Gonds of Adilabad, played with the fingers of both hands. It is one of the sacred Dandari drums, worshipped at the start of the festival, and gives its name to a lively Dandari dance.

Telangana
Drum

Tappeta

A small, flat, round drum tied to the chest and beaten with both hands by the dancers of Tappeta Gullu, the devotional dance of the Golla (Yadava) herding community of Srikakulam, Vizianagaram and Visakhapatnam in North Coastal Andhra. In everyday Telugu the word tappeta is also used for the dappu.

Andhra Pradesh
Drum

Tudumu

A kettle drum of the tribal communities of Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, described as a hemispherical clay bowl with goat skin stretched over the top and beaten with leather straps. It accompanies the Dhimsa dance of the Araku valley and the Gussadi dances of the Raj Gonds of Adilabad.

Drum

Vette (Turbuli)

A small, shallow drum of the Raj Gonds of Adilabad, made from a clay, iron or wooden bowl covered with skin and struck with two thin bamboo sticks. One of the Dandari festival drums, it gives a sharp, clicking rhythm; according to a Gond legend and a custom recorded in the 1940s, a new vette is touched by a menstruating woman so that it sounds right.

Wind instrument

Kalikom

A long, curved horn of the Gonds of Adilabad, played by Pardhan musicians. It sounds together with the tudum drum and the pepre shawm in the Dandari–Gussadi festival and at the Nagoba Jatara, and its call is likened to a conch summoning people to battle.

Telangana

Wind instrument

Pepre

A shawm of the sannayi type played by the Pardhan musicians of the Raj Gonds in Adilabad. Played in pairs with the kikri fiddle and drums, it sounds at Gond clan-god rites, in the Dandari–Gussadi festival and at the Nagoba Jatara.

Telangana
Wind instrument

Pillana Grovi

The pillana grovi is the Telugu name for the simple bamboo flute, the instrument of Krishna. Village flutes of this kind were widely played in the Godavari region, and Telugu ballads describe herders playing them to call their calves.

Andhra PradeshTelangana
Wind instrument

Sannayi (Nadaswaram)

Sannayi is the everyday Telugu name for the nadaswaram, the long double-reed pipe whose sound is considered auspicious. Played with the dolu drum, it accompanies weddings, temple rituals, village festivals and the Sankranti rounds of the Gangireddu bull performers.

String instruments తంత్రీ వాద్యాలు

String instrument

Burra Veena

A three-stringed instrument made from a dried gourd and a bamboo stick and played with little sticks hung with bells, used to accompany tatvalu and devotional songs. Its last known player, Dasari Kondappa of Damaragidda in Narayanpet district, received the Padma Shri in 2024.

Telangana
String instrument

Kikri (Kingri)

A three-stringed bowed fiddle with a square, skin-covered wooden sound box, played by the Pardhan and Thoti bards of the Gonds in the Adilabad region. It accompanies Gond hymns and epics and clan-god rites, and is treated as sacred, personified as the ancestress Hirabai.

Telangana
String instrument

Kinnera

A veena-like string instrument with a bamboo neck and dried bottle-gourd resonators, played by Dakkali bards (and once by the Chenchus of the Nallamala forests) to accompany ballads of Telangana's folk heroes. Its best-known player, Darshanam Mogilaiah, plays a 12-fret version and received the Padma Shri in 2022.

Telangana
String instrument

Tambura

The shoulder-held string drone of Telugu storytellers: the Burrakatha narrator plucks it while keeping time with metal rings (andelu), and the Sarada singers of Telangana call the same instrument 'Sarada'. It gave Burrakatha its name, from the burra or gourd shell of the tambura.

Bells, cymbals & clappers

Andelu

Andelu are hollow brass rings with small metal balls inside, worn on the thumb and fingers of a storyteller's hand. The Burrakatha narrator clinks them in time while strumming his tambura, and Jangam and Oggu Katha storytellers use them the same way to keep the beat.

Andhra PradeshTelangana
Bells, cymbals & clappers

Chekkalu (Chekka Bhajana clappers)

Chekkalu are the pair of foot-long, carved wooden clappers with ringing metal discs that give Chekka Bhajana its name. Dancers moving in a circle clap them in one hand while singing devotional songs and Ramayana stories, a tradition especially associated with Rayalaseema.

Andhra Pradesh
Bells, cymbals & clappers

Chirutalu

Chirutalu are a pair of wooden clappers, often fitted with jingles or finger rings, that a singer clicks in one hand to keep time. Harikatha storytellers and Sankranti Haridasus carry small ones, while village bhajana troupes and Telangana's Chirutala Ramayanam use larger, carved pairs.

Andhra PradeshTelangana
Bells, cymbals & clappers

Kolatam Karralu (Kolatam sticks)

Kolatam karralu are the short, polished wooden sticks that dancers strike together, against their own and their partners' sticks, in the Kolatam stick dance. Traditional sticks are shaped from hard timber, taper at both ends and may carry small bells and jingling rings.

Andhra PradeshTelangana
Bells, cymbals & clappers

Talalu

Talalu are small cymbals of bronze or other bell metal, joined by a cord and clashed together to mark the beat. They keep time in village bhajanas and in Telangana's Oggu Katha and Chindu Bhagavatam, and larger talalu ring out in forms such as the Kola Sambaram of the Godavari districts.
