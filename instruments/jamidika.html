జమిడిక

Jamidika (Jamuku)

Also known as Jamuku (north coastal Andhra; the drum of Jamukula Katha) (జముకు), Jamidike (జమిడికె), Jimilika / Jamilika (జిమిలిక), Samidika (సమిడిక), Javanika (older name) (జవనిక), Bavanika (బవనిక), Budiki (Srikakulam) (బుడికి), Bonela kunda (parts of Srikakulam) (బోనెల కుండ), Pamba (in some areas) (పంబ)

A single-headed 'string drum' whose skin is sounded through a gut string that the player pulls and plucks. Called jamidika by the Baindla priest-singers of Telangana and jamuku in north coastal Andhra, it accompanies the stories of Yellamma and other village goddesses.

About

The jamidika (జమిడిక) is the ritual instrument of the Baindlas, also called Bavanis, the hereditary priests and story-singers of the Madiga community in Telangana.1,5,6 Mikkilineni Radhakrishna Murthy writes that over time the Baindla jamidika came to be called jimilika and jamuku, and that anyone who has seen today's jamuku can picture the jamidika.1 Jamuku is the name used in north coastal Andhra, where the instrument gives its name to Jamukula Katha and is also called jamilika or budiki.2,7

The Baindlas are known by the instrument. Bheemaiah quotes Sri Rama Raju's view that the Bavanis got their name from the bavanika they play, and notes that the bavanika is also called pamba; in Telangana Baindlas are also called Bavaneelavaru, Pambalavaru and Jamukulavaru. He calls the jamidika a cultural icon without which Baindla culture is unimaginable.5,8

The instrument has a long history. Mikkilineni quotes a verse from the Telugu work Kridabhiramam praising a Bavani singer who sang the stories of Parashurama before the goddess Ekavira with masterly playing, as evidence that Bavanis were already prominent in the Kakatiya period, and he writes that the javanika - 'today's jamuku' - accompanied their Yellamma stories with its 'jukajum, jukajum' sound.1,2,4

How it is made

The body is a hollow shell open at one end, described as shaped like a kuncham (a tapering grain measure) or as conical, and made of brass or wood; some are of bronze.1,2,5 Sakshi describes the Baindla jamidika of Telangana as made of brass.6 The closed end is covered with a thin skin: sources variously name the tender skin of a calf, a thin membrane-like buffalo skin, or frog skin.5,2,7,9

A small hole is made in the centre of the skin and a gut string - traditionally an animal sinew - is knotted and passed through it into the body. At the open end the string is tied to a short piece of wood or a small wooden roller, which may carry a bunch of small bells.2,5,7 Newer jamukus are also made with rubber, wood, tin sheet, bronze or brass.7,9

According to the Sphoorthi Theatre account, early instruments were earthen pots covered with the belly skin of a dead ox, with the sinew of its spinal cord used as the string; wood and later copper, brass or bronze replaced the pot for durability.10

How it is played

The player holds the body against the side or under the arm, pulls the stick at the back to draw the string taut, and plucks or strums the string with the fingertips; tightening and loosening the pull changes the sound that comes from the skin.2,5,6,9 The rhythm follows the tune of each song and is played forcefully at dramatic moments.1,6

In a Baindla performance the lead narrator sings in the middle while companions (vantas) beside him play jamidikas and sing the refrains.1,6 In Mahabubnagar district two Baindla artists perform together, the narrator with a large jamidika and his partner with a smaller one tuned to a different note; in Nalgonda and Khammam districts the lead performer plays the jamidika while others add a small harmonium, cymbals and a small drum.5 Baindlas play differently for weddings and for deaths.1

In Jamukula Katha of north coastal Andhra there are three performers - the narrator in the middle, one companion for humour and one for commentary - and the two companions play jamukus. In the budiki form of Srikakulam district, two budikis (jamukus) and the ankle bells are the only instruments, and the players hang the budikis from the armpit.2

Beliefs & practices

The jamidika is believed to hold power. A myth recorded by Tangirala Venkata Subba Rao and cited by Bheemaiah says that when the goddess Renuka hid from her son Parashurama in a trough in the Madiga quarter, he came disguised as a Pambala playing the jamidika and prayed to her, and at its sound she came out; Baindlas hold a strong conviction about their instrument.5

Jamuku artists of north Andhra tell their own origin story: after killing the demon Suketu, the goddess Yellamma had the skin of his back stretched over a box, his spinal nerve made into the single string and the marrow of his leg into a small fitting, and so the jamuku was made. Villagers also believe that no evil wind, ghost or spirit can come as far as the jamuku's sound carries, and those who take up Jamukula Katha as a profession regard it as sacred.7

Baindlas serve as priests for the Madigas, conducting their weddings and mediating with village goddesses such as Yellamma, Maisamma and Pochamma; they believe that any lapse in this mediation can anger the goddesses and bring diseases such as plague and cholera on the village.5,6 Mikkilineni adds that Baindlas perform only for Shaiva households, at weddings and at deaths.1 Telugu Wikipedia notes that, because of untouchability, others would not touch the jamidike played by the Baindlas.8

Baindla children learn the jamidika, cymbals and harmonium by travelling with their parents and imitating them, and elders fear that children who study further will forget the family occupation.5

Where you will hear it

In Telangana, Baindla artists play the jamidika while telling the stories of Renuka Yellamma, Pochamma, Maisamma and Poleramma at village goddess festivals. After bonalu offerings are made they stay at the shrine through the night, draw patterns (patnams) with turmeric and vermilion, and narrate the goddesses' histories until dawn in costumes such as Parashurama, Mandhata and Potharaju.6 They also narrate the Yellamma story at local celebrations and Madiga weddings.5

In north coastal Andhra (Srikakulam, Vizianagaram and Visakhapatnam districts) and in the Godavari districts, Jamukula Katha - also called Bonela paata or Budiki paata - is performed by Mala and Madiga artists, with stories from the epics and local ballads.2,7,9 In the 1940s the Praja Natya Mandali took up the form for social themes, and the poet-activist Subbarao Panigrahi of Sompeta composed many Jamukula Katha songs during the Srikakulam tribal peasant struggle.7,11

In Rayalaseema and Telangana the Asadulu, a priestly group among the Madigas, play their javanikas vigorously at village goddess festivals and sing the Yellamma story.3 Mikkilineni also notes that Bavanis once told the Renuka (Yellamma) story over two nights while playing the javanika.4

The tradition is under strain. Sakshi reports that only a few Baindla story-singers remain in Telangana, finding work mainly in the Ashadha and Shravana months, and that the younger generation is not coming forward to learn; Bheemaiah describes the Baindla occupation as being on the verge of extinction.6,5
