కిక్రి

Kikri (Kingri)

Also known as Kingri (కింగ్రి), Khikri, Kikiri, Hirabai Kikri (హీరాబాయి కిక్రి), Dunadi vaja (sacred stringed instrument)

A three-stringed bowed fiddle with a square, skin-covered wooden sound box, played by the Pardhan and Thoti bards of the Gonds in the Adilabad region. It accompanies Gond hymns and epics and clan-god rites, and is treated as sacred, personified as the ancestress Hirabai.

About

The kikri, also written kingri or khikri, is the fiddle of the Pardhans, the hereditary bards of the Raj Gonds of the Adilabad region in northern Telangana, and of the Thotis, a smaller community of Gond bards. The anthropologist Christoph von Fürer-Haimendorf, who studied the Adilabad Gonds in the 1940s, called the kingri 'the most important possession of a Pardhan'1,2. Telugu sources describe it as an instrument widely known in Gond society, on which Pardhans and Thotis sing the traditional stories of the Gond tribe3,4.

The songs and stories of the Pardhans are the main store of Gond oral tradition. At the rites for each clan god (Persa Pen) the Pardhan attached to that god plays the fiddle and quietly sings the ancient hymns, and afterwards recites the myths of the origin of the Gonds and of the clan deities with two assistants1.

Telugu writing compares the kikri with other folk fiddles of India, such as the ravanahatha of Rajasthan, and notes that although it was once found in several states where Gonds live, today it is used mainly by Gondi-speaking people in Telangana and Maharashtra3.

How it is made

The kikri has a square wooden sound box covered with a skin membrane. A long bamboo neck runs through the box, and three tuning pegs are set at the other end of the bamboo1,2,4. The Hindu reported that the three strings and the hair of the bow are made of horse hair2.

A Telugu description drawn from the book Mooladhwani says the resonator, about one and a half spans long, is made by hollowing out a piece of tree trunk and covering it with goat skin; a bamboo is pushed through it, and three holes at the other end hold tuning pegs called pootilu3.

The bow is curved and hung with small bells (gajjelu) that jingle in time with the tune as it is drawn2,3. Haimendorf's drawing of a Pardhan's kingri shows a bow made of antelope horn, and he described the tiny bells on a bow of antelope horn and horse hair jingling softly as the musician played1.

Instruments are kept for generations. In 2015 the Pardhan Mesram Tukdoji told The Hindu that his kingri was at least 200 years old and that its strings had not been replaced since he inherited it 52 years earlier2.

How it is played

The kikri is played seated, held upright and bowed. Mesram Tukdoji explained that the instrument rests on the left shoulder while it is played2. The Telugu description says the player presses the box with his chin and draws the bow across the strings much as a violin is played3.

Haimendorf recorded how the Pardhan Chitru performed the Gond creation epic after a Persa Pen feast. After tuning his three-stringed fiddle he played a short prelude, then sang, with his son and nephew joining in at the end of each verse while the son kept rhythm on a small pair of cymbals. Sung verses alternated with spoken declamation and short passages on the fiddle, and during the chorus he played louder and more vigorously than when accompanying his own solo1.

At festivals it is usually the most prominent Pardhan who plays the kingri, while younger men blow trumpets and beat drums1,4. According to the Telugu account, the kikri is played together with a pair of shawms called pepre and a skin drum called dakki3.

Beliefs & practices

The kikri is regarded as sacred. In a Gond origin myth recorded by Haimendorf, when the fire goddess threatened Pahandi Kupar Lingal and the Gonds, Hirabai, the mother of the young Hira who was sent to help them, turned herself into a kingri so that she could rest on her son's shoulder as his guardian. As his bow moved 'ki-ki-ki' over the strings, the goddess was spellbound and the rites could continue. In another version the danger came from the untamed clan god Persa Pen, and Haimendorf wrote that most Gonds believed the Pardhan's playing on the kingri during Persa Pen rites was necessary to tame the fierce god and protect the worshippers1.

Present-day accounts keep this identity. Telugu sources call the instrument Hirabai kikri and associate it with Hira Sukka, regarded as the religious and musical teacher of the Pardhans and the Gonds3. Mesram Tukdoji told The Hindu that the kingri is female and is called Hirabai, and that when it is not in use it is tied to its male counterpart, Heerasukka, which is shaped like a small sword2.

Customs surround its handling. According to Tukdoji, the kingri may be played only by married men2. At a Persa Pen feast Haimendorf saw the Pardhan Chitru sacrifice a small chicken in front of his fiddle, which, according to myth, is the transformed body of his ancestress Hirabai; and in a Gond legend he recorded, the wise Pardhan Hirasuka first washes the 'leg' of his fiddle Hirabai before washing his own feet1.

The Hindu also reported a belief that gods who were angry with the tribe could be pacified only when Pardhan elders played the 'Dunadi vaja', the sacred stringed instrument2.

Where you will hear it

The kikri is played during Persa Pen clan-god rites and other religious ceremonies, and at rites connected with births, marriages and deaths among the Gonds1,2,5. A list of Gond instruments compiled in Adilabad notes that the kikri is played during worship of the gods and at the time of death6.

It is prominent at the Nagoba Jatara of the Mesram clan of Gonds at Keslapur in Indervelli mandal, Adilabad district, where The Hindu photographed the Pardhan Mesram Tukdoji playing the kingri at the Nagoba temple and described the chorus that he and his team sing to its accompaniment during the jatara2.

The Thotis, like the Pardhans, sing Gond history at annual jataras, recite stories and folk songs, and perform with the kingri at marriages and funerals; Haimendorf noted that stories of the god Bhimana are their special repertoire1,5.

Haimendorf observed in the 1940s that where Pardhans gave up their fiddle and their old association with the Gonds, cultural life suffered1. A 2025 study of the Thoti community found that the playing of the kikri and related instruments by Thoti men at festivals and funerals in Gond homes has greatly declined, although the community still plays the kikri5. The instrument has been shown in exhibitions of tribal instruments in Hyderabad, including Adya Kala's 'Adi Dhwani'7.

Research notes: points where sources differ or could not be confirmed
  • Spelling varies between sources: kikri, kingri, khikri, kikiri and keekri; Telugu sources use both కిక్రి and కింగ్రి.
  • Posture: The Hindu says the kingri rests on the left shoulder, while the Telugu Wikipedia summary of Mooladhwani says the box is pressed with the chin. These may describe the same upright hold, but this was not confirmed.
  • Some older literature calls the Pardhans priests of the Gonds; Haimendorf stressed that in Adilabad they act only as musicians and bards, while other sources say they sometimes serve as priests.
  • In Haimendorf's myth Hirabai is the mother of Hira (Hirasuka), the first Pardhan; Telugu Wikipedia calls Hira Sukka the Pardhans' teacher and ties the kikri to him. The two tellings differ in detail.
  • The term 'Dunadi vaja' appears only in The Hindu (2015); its exact Gondi form and use were not confirmed.
  • Online summaries sometimes mix up the kikri with the bana, the Pardhan fiddle of central India; how the two names relate was not researched.
  • No source was found on who makes kikris today or on any ritual connected with making one.

Quick facts

TypeThree-stringed bowed fiddle
MaterialsSquare wooden sound box covered with skin (goat skin in Telugu accounts), bamboo neck, horse-hair strings and bow hair, bells on the bow
Played byPardhan and Thoti bards of the Gonds; according to one Pardhan elder, only married men
Played withA bow hung with small bells; alongside pepre shawms, drums (including the dakki) and small cymbals
RegionAdilabad region of northern Telangana; also Gondi-speaking areas of Maharashtra
BeliefPersonified as the ancestress Hirabai; its music is believed to calm the clan god Persa Pen
CommunitiesPardhan (bards of the Gonds), Thoti, Raj Gond (patrons)
