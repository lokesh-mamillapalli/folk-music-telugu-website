కిన్నెర

Kinnera

Also known as 12-step kinnera (12-mettu kinnera) (12 మెట్ల కిన్నెర), Kinneri, Kinnara

A veena-like string instrument with a bamboo neck and dried bottle-gourd resonators, played by Dakkali bards (and once by the Chenchus of the Nallamala forests) to accompany ballads of Telangana's folk heroes. Its best-known player, Darshanam Mogilaiah, plays a 12-fret version and received the Padma Shri in 2022.

About

The kinnera (కిన్నెర) is a stringed instrument native to nomadic communities of the Deccan plateau such as the Dakkali and the Chenchu. A kinnera performance combines singing and playing, and its ballads are sung mainly in rustic Telugu1. The scholar Jayadhir Tirumala Rao traces its origins to around the 4th century CE in the Deccan, and says the Dakkali of the Mahabubnagar region, near the Nallamala forest, have performed with it since at least the 12th century1.

Much like the Saraswati veena, it is made from natural materials: a bamboo neck with resonators of sun-dried, hollowed bottle gourds. Kinneras are made with seven, nine, 12 or 13 frets; larger instruments have three gourd resonators and smaller ones two1,3. It has also been described as a folk veena that wandering bards used to narrate community histories and folklore11.

The best-known player today is Darshanam Mogilaiah (also spelled Mogulaiah) of Nagarkurnool district, whose kinnera has 12 frets, or steps (mettu). He received the Padma Shri in 2022, cited as the last player to have mastered the 12 steps of the instrument8,4.

How it is made

Accounts of the traditional instrument describe a bamboo neck, dried and hollowed gourds as resonators, strings of human hair or animal nerve, and frets made of pangolin scales fixed in place with honey-wax (beeswax)3,2. The strings were also once made of women's hair and horse-tail hair, but these have long been replaced by thin metal strings1.

The right gourd is essential. According to Jayadhir Tirumala Rao, the Chenchus lost the instrument about half a century ago when the gourd used for its resonator became extinct in their region, and shrinking forests have made suitable gourds and pangolins hard to obtain. Making the kinnera, like playing it, requires special skills3,1.

Mogilaiah's family has made its own kinneras for generations5. His two-stringed instrument is described as made from bamboo, the dried outer shell of round bottle gourds, honeycomb, wax, bull horn, beads, mirrors and peacock feathers, and its strings of goat nerve have been replaced with metal ones4,8. In 2014 the researcher Dasari Ranga explained that Mogilaiah fixes pieces of dried fruit, coconut or dried horn at 12 places on the instrument so that its two strings give different tunes6.

Mogilaiah's forefathers made kinneras with seven steps and his father made one with nine. The elders told him a kinnera could not have more than eleven steps, but over time he built one with twelve5. His instrument is elaborately painted and decorated, and carries a small wooden turtle puppet that bows, jumps and salutes the audience2.

A 1992 survey of Telugu folk arts describes a simpler tribal kinnera: a hollow bamboo about 20 inches long with four frets fixed to its middle with wax, two strings stretched over the frets, and a resonator at one end of the bamboo9.

How it is played

The kinnera player accompanies his or her own singing. The ballads are interspersed with short, often dramatic spoken passages, and the performer's tone of voice, facial expressions and body language change with the mood of the song1.

Describing Dakkali Balamma in her eighties, Scroll wrote that her kinnera produced a melodious, sharp sound as she strummed it, and that her grasp of shruti (pitch) and laya (rhythm) remained firm1. Mogilaiah is known for making up songs within minutes on subjects ranging from people to nature5.

Beliefs & practices

The Dakkali are a Dalit sub-caste of troubadours described as dependents of the Madigas. Historically one section of the Dakkali sang the Jambava (Jamba) Puranam, which explained their subservience to their chief patrons the Madigas, while another section played the kinnera. Both lived on alms from the Madigas, who rewarded performers with money, food and clothes2,1,5.

A story told among the tribes explains why the Chenchus gave up the instrument: a woman cutting vegetables was so lost in the music of her husband's kinnera that she cut up her infant as well, and the horrified tribe banned the instrument from the hills2,1.

According to Jayadhir Tirumala Rao, when the Dakkali community inherited the kinnera it reduced the number of frets to seven, purportedly in honour of the Chenchus3.

The art is passed down within families. Mogilaiah is the fifth generation of kinnera players in his family and learned from his father Yellaiah, travelling with him from the age of eight. Balamma learned from her father and remembered travelling from village to village on donkeys to play and sing5,4,2.

Mogilaiah has described the instrument's place in village life: his family did not beg while playing but performed wherever they were asked, sang through the night in a house where someone had died to console the bereaved, and sang for people bitten by snakes, saying that along with medicine the songs helped cure them4. He also says the kinnera has no caste: besides the Dakkali and Chenchu, he has seen a man of the Tenugu caste practise it, knew Muslim families who performed it, and has heard that even Nawabs made and played it5.

The name kinnera also appears in older Telugu literature. According to the 1992 survey, Krishnadevaraya's 16th-century poem Amuktamalyada lists a 'buruma kinnera' among the instruments of its day, and describes Dasari singers of the Vijayanagara period, who served as gurus and priests to the Mala and Madiga communities, singing songs to a kinnera called 'bandalika' as they went about seeking alms13.

Where you will hear it

Kinnera ballads celebrate local heroes remembered for helping the poor, such as Pandugolla (Panduga) Sayanna, an 18th-century figure, Miya Saab (Meera Saab), Endavetla Fakeeraiah, Bandolla Kurumanna and Vettem Ranganayakamma, as well as stories of royal families2,4,5,7. Songs from the Jamba Puranam are also sung with the kinnera1,11.

The tradition is centred on the old Mahabubnagar region of southern Telangana. The last performers are reported in Mahabubnagar and Vikarabad districts, with possibly a few more in Karnataka, Adilabad district and the Gond areas of Madhya Pradesh1. A 1992 account also records that Gondhali storytellers of the Aare community in Telangana are said to have once told their stories with a maddela drum and a kinnera, though no such troupes remained10.

In August 2015 a team from the Telangana Rachayithala Vedika and the University of Hyderabad, including Jayadhir Tirumala Rao and G. Manoja of Palamuru University, took the Dakkali performer Pochayya and his kinnera to Appapur in the Nallamala forests on the International Day of the World's Indigenous Peoples. Elderly Chenchus recognised the instrument, and a few played it from memory after nearly fifty years1,3.

Mogilaiah reached a wide audience when he sang the title song of the Telugu film Bheemla Nayak (2022), which draws on the Miya Saab folklore he had sung for decades4.

The kinnera is close to extinction. In 2019 Scroll estimated that only about a dozen people in India still played it, and Dakkali Balamma, the only known woman performer, died in December 20181,2. Mogilaiah has received the Telangana government's Ugadi Puraskaram (2015), a monthly pension and a lesson in the Class 8 textbook, but an effort to teach the kinnera at Potti Sreeramulu Telugu University found few takers1,2,5. A 2021 review of the study Mula Dhwani notes that only one person can still play the 12-step kinnera12.

Research notes: points where sources differ or could not be confirmed
  • Community of Darshanam Mogilaiah: The News Minute, The Indian Express and the Ministry of Tribal Affairs describe him as from a Madiga (Dalit) family; Scroll calls him part of the Dakkali tribe (the Dakkali are described as a Madiga sub-caste).
  • Spellings vary between sources: Mogilaiah/Mogulaiah (Telugu మొగిలయ్య/మొగులయ్య); Pochayya/Pochaiah; Pandugolla/Panduga/Pandaga Sayanna; Endavetla/Endematla/Endamatla Fakeeraiah; Miya Saab/Meera Saab/Mir Saab (Sakshi: మీర్‌సాబ్); Bandolla Kurumanna/Kurmanna.
  • Number of strings: The Indian Express and the Ministry of Tribal Affairs call Mogilaiah's kinnera two-stringed, while the 1992 Mikkilineni survey and The Hans India call the kinnera multi-stringed (and the same survey then describes a two-string, four-fret tribal kinnera without naming the tribe).
  • Fret materials on Mogilaiah's 12-step kinnera are not clearly documented: pangolin scales are described for older instruments, while sources list bull horn among his materials and Dasari Ranga (Times of India, 2014) mentions dried fruit, coconut or horn fixed at 12 places.
  • 'Honey-wax' / 'honey wax' / Telugu 'మైనం' in the sources has been rendered as beeswax.
  • The 4th-century origin and 12th-century Dakkali dates are claims by Jayadhir Tirumala Rao as quoted by Scroll; they were not independently confirmed.
  • Fret counts: The Hindu (2015) says the Dakkali reduced the frets to seven in honour of the Chenchus; The News Minute says Mogilaiah's forefathers used seven and his father nine; The Hindu (2017) describes a 13-fret Chenchu instrument in Haimendorf's photograph; at the 2015 Appapur visit a blind Chenchu man said he was used to a 12-fret version (Scroll).
  • Dakkali Balamma's age: The Hindu (August 2017) says she was 95, while Scroll (2019) says she was 86 about four years earlier.
  • The Gondhali (Aare) use of a kinnera is reported as hearsay ('it is said') in the 1992 survey.
  • The kinnera named in Amuktamalyada ('buruma kinnera', 'bandalika') is known here only through the 1992 survey's summary; whether it was the same instrument as the Dakkali and Chenchu kinnera is not established.
  • None of the sources consulted gives a date of death for Mogilaiah; the Ministry of Tribal Affairs page is written in the past tense, so his current status was not assumed either way.
