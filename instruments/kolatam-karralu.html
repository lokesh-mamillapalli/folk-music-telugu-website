Kolatam Karralu (Kolatam sticks)

Also known as Kolalu (కోలలు), Kolata karralu (కోలాట కర్రలు), Kolatapu chirutalu (కోలాటపు చిరుతలు), Kola (కోల)

Kolatam karralu are the short, polished wooden sticks that dancers strike together, against their own and their partners' sticks, in the Kolatam stick dance. Traditional sticks are shaped from hard timber, taper at both ends and may carry small bells and jingling rings.

About

Kolatam (kola, 'stick', and ata, 'play' or 'dance') is a group song-dance in which performers strike short sticks together in rhythm while moving in circles1,2. C. P. Brown's dictionary (1903) defines kolatamu as a dance in a ring in which sticks are struck together in harmony, and notes that a single stick is also called by this name3. The sticks, called kolalu or kolatam karralu, are both the dancers' prop and the main percussion instrument of the dance1,4.

Kolatam has a long recorded history. Mikkilineni Radhakrishna Murthy notes that Palkuriki Somanatha refers to 'kolata godiya' in his Panditaradhya Charitra, and that sculptures of women dancing Kolatam can still be seen on the walls of the Vijayanagara ruins and on the enclosure walls of the Srisailam temple1.

How it is made

Mikkilineni, drawing on the research of folklorist Bittu Venkateswarlu, describes Kolatam sticks as thin at the start, swelling in the middle and thin again at the end, with a grip for holding. The sticks are chosen from strong, heartwood-rich (cheva) timbers such as billudu, chandra, epe, rela, teak and egisa, and are carefully finished1. In Brown's dictionary cheva means the heart or core of a tree5, chandra is an acacia6 and rela is the Indian laburnum, Cassia fistula7.

Jingles are added for sound: andelu (jingling rings) are fixed near the grip and gajjelu (small bells) at the end of each stick, which gives a sweet sound while playing1. The sticks are brightly coloured; each player is described carrying colourful kola sticks1, and a 2014 Deccan Chronicle report from Adilabad describes 'special decorated sticks, kolalu' used just as in Dandiya4.

How it is played

Players first stand in a circle and pray to their chosen deity. They then stand in pairs facing each other and strike the sticks rhythmically; when the leader calls out a rhythmic phrase to raise the speed, the circle divides into inner and outer rings. Each pair (uddi) strikes sticks with each other, and the leader sets the rhythm with syllables that vary from region to region, such as 'taka taka tayyaku diddimi' or 'kolu kolu kolanna'1.

Each song with its movement pattern is called a kopu. A kopu starts with the song and dance together (ettugada), shifts from the second to the third speed in an energetic section called usettu, where the players leap and sing, and is brought to a stop by the leader's closing phrase (muktayimpu)1. The troupe usually has twenty to forty players, always in even numbers, led by a leader known as Kolanna pantulu, pennuddi or ayyavaru, and all the players tie gajjelu to their ankles1.

In jada kopu (braid) Kolatam, an even number of ropes is tied to a high branch, often of a tamarind tree, and each dancer holds one rope; moving in and out in two groups, they weave the ropes into a braid while singing and then unweave it by dancing in reverse. A single wrong step tangles the braid, which makes this the most demanding form of Kolatam1,2. A similar rope form, pinnal kolattam, is known in Tamil Nadu8.

Beliefs & practices

Kolatam is devotional as well as recreational. In villages it is played at night during leisure time; troupes carry a pole of the deity from house to house and perform before each home, and they also perform at big fairs, temple festivals and in the streets1. Telugu Wikipedia notes performances at the jataras of village goddesses such as Uradamma, Gandi Maisamma, Gangadevi, Katta Maisamma, Pothalingamma and Poleramma2.

Discipline is part of the tradition. The leader decides the place and time of performance, the players follow the guru's word, and the leader is expected to be healthy and expert in rhythm, tala, songs and play; in some areas the leader carries a whip on his shoulder and cracks it to warn those who break discipline1. During the fast sections, coloured powder, flowers, sandal paste and rose water are sprinkled over the dancers to spur them on1.

Kolatam also has a place in festivals and schooling. In Telangana it is performed during Holi and during Bathukamma Navaratri, when it is compared with the Dandiya of Durga Navaratri4. Mikkilineni recalls that girls' jada kopu Kolatam was once taught as an exercise in evening drill classes, and that Telangana Kolatam songs include dialogues depicting quarrels between husband and wife1.

Where you will hear it

Kolatam is performed across Andhra Pradesh and Telangana. Names of kopus vary by region; in the Palnadu area they include the prarthana (prayer), uddi tirugudu, Krishna, Kamsa, jada, Bharata, bhama, lali and lanke kopus1.

In Adilabad district, Kolatam artists from Deepaidguda village in Jainad mandal performed at the collectorate in 2014, and Jada Koppula Aata, a related traditional dance, was reported to be popular in only a few villages in the eastern part of the district4. Kolatam songs there are sung by elders to harmonium and talalu, while in the traditional form described by Mikkilineni musicians in the centre of the circle play maddela, harmonium and tappeta4,1.

Research notes: points where sources differ or could not be confirmed
  • Mikkilineni also states that the foreign traveller Abdul Razzaq described Kolatam performances in the Vijayanagara empire; this could not be checked against Abdul Razzaq's own account and is left out of the main text.
  • Botanical identities of billudu, epe and egisa are not given by the source. Brown's 1903 dictionary identifies epe as the sal tree, but modern usage may differ, so no English names are given for these woods.
  • Stick length: English Wikipedia's Kolattam article (largely about Tamil Nadu) gives about 1.5 ft; no Telugu source giving measurements was found.
  • Mikkilineni's list of Kolatam accompaniment includes 'తాలు', probably a misprint for తాళాలు (talalu); only the Deccan Chronicle report is used for talalu.
  • Online shops sell lacquered 'kolatam sticks' from the Etikoppaka and Kondapalli toy-making traditions, but no reliable source was found documenting these crafts as traditional makers of Kolatam sticks. No making rituals for the sticks were found.
  • The Deccan Chronicle report calls Kolatam 'unique to Telangana', although Kolatam is also documented in Andhra Pradesh and Tamil Nadu.
