మద్దెల

Maddela

Also known as Maddala (variant form) (మద్దల), Mardala (Sanskrit source word) (మర్దళ), Mridangam (treated as a synonym in dictionaries) (మృదంగము)

The Telugu name for a mridangam-type skin drum that accompanies song in folk theatre: Tholu Bommalata shadow puppetry, Chindu Bhagavatam, Yakshaganam and street-theatre troupes, daytime impersonators, kolatam and bhajans, usually together with harmonium and cymbals.

About

Maddela (మద్దెల) is the Telugu form of the Sanskrit word mardala. Telugu dictionaries define it as a skin drum played to accompany singing and list mridangamu, murajamu and mardalamu among its synonyms; C. P. Brown's dictionary (1903) derives it from Sanskrit mṛdaṅga and glosses it 'a small drum', and Nalimela Bhaskar's Telangana Padakosam (2010) glosses it simply as mridangam.1,2

The word is old in Telugu literature. Vavilla's dictionary (1949) quotes the Ranganatha Ramayanam, where the maddela sounds 'dimi dimi' as actresses dance to songs, and Srinatha's Palnati Veera Charitra describes a court dancer who ties on her ankle bells and takes her place 'between the maddela and the cymbals' before she begins to dance.1,3

Mikkilineni Radhakrishna Murthy points to older evidence as well: sculptures in a temple at the eastern end of the Ramappa tank bund at Palampet, from the Kakatiya period, showing women playing the maddela while dancers perform, and a verse of Hala's Prakrit anthology Gatha Saptashati (3.53), which in the Telugu rendering he quotes says that the maddela sounds sweet only as long as there is food (rice) on its face and grumbles harshly without it.4,5

How it is made

The sources consulted give little detail on how folk maddelas are made. Dictionaries classify the maddela as a charma vadyam (an instrument sounded on a stretched skin) and equate it with the mridangam, and the Gatha Saptashati verse quoted by Mikkilineni refers to food applied to the drum's face to give it a sweet tone.1,5

The maddela is also used as a yardstick in descriptions of tribal drums: M. A. Rauf, quoted by Mikkilineni, describes the Savara daggudu as shaped like a maddela, and the para of the Raj Gonds as like a maddela but two feet long, with goat skin on both ends played with the fingers.6

How it is played

The maddela is an accompanying drum, normally heard with a harmonium for the drone (sruti) and cymbals (talalu). In Tholu Bommalata, the singers, the harmonium player, the cymbal player, the maddela player and the person making sound effects all sit behind the screen, and the instrumentalists also sing the chorus (vanta); the players also stamp on wooden boards under their feet in battle scenes and when chariots or horses run.7

A 2025 research paper in the Telugu journal Auchithyam likewise names maddelu, harmonium and cymbals as the instruments of traditional puppet troupes, the Telugu Wikipedia article on Tholu Bommalata lists harmonium, cymbals, tokkudu billa (foot clappers), maddela, dappu and ankle bells, and the MAP Academy's encyclopedia lists the 'muddalam and mridangam' among Tholu Bommalata's percussion, with cymbals, harmonium, mukhaveena and conch.8,9,10

In Chindu Bhagavatam of Telangana, the Chindu Bhagavatulu dance their steps to maddelu, cymbals, ankle bells, harmonium, a gourd pipe (poongi) and sannayi, according to a 2024 article by Osmania University researcher Bashipangu Venkanna. Mikkilineni lists mridangam, cymbals, ankle bells and harmonium as the main instruments of the Chindu women's performances, with the actors singing the chorus from the background and women playing the cymbals.11,12

Skill on the drum was valued: Mikkilineni notes that the celebrated pagati veshagadu (daytime impersonator) Bhagavatula Kumaraswamy, who could play thirty-two different roles, was famous for his maddela playing and could reproduce the maddela wonderfully with his mouth.13

Beliefs & practices

Because it accompanies devotional theatre, the maddela is part of ritual as well as entertainment. A Tholu Bommalata show opens with a mangala harati sung in raga Todi and a prayer to Vighneswara so that no obstacles arise, and a Chindu Bhagotam begins with prayers to Ganapati and Saraswati.7,11

Mikkilineni records that the Chindu performers play an 'Ellamma ata' in every village where they perform, praying that the village stay green and prosperous, and that people in many villages of Nizamabad district still believed this performance keeps famine away.12

Where you will hear it

Mikkilineni's survey shows the maddela across Telugu folk theatre. The Yanadi Bhagavatam troupes of coastal Andhra, which staged Usha Parinayam, Krishna leelalu, Savitri and Sasirekha Parinayam through the night, used the maddela, cymbals and a harmonium. In Rayalaseema, troupes staging mythological plays in the village bazaar would first make a festive noise with maddela and cymbals.14,15

In Telangana, the Gondhali performers who play street bhagotams in villages of the Are community use harmonium and maddela as accompaniment, and in earlier Gondhali Burrakathas one man played the maddela, another the kinnera and a third the cymbals. The Gante Bhagavatulu (Telaga Dasaris) sing their kirtanas to a harmonium drone with maddela and cymbals.16,17

The Kuchipudi Bhagavatulu's daytime impersonations (pagati veshalu), such as the Ardhanarishvara vesham, were accompanied by at least two Bhagavatulu playing harmonium and maddela, and kolatam groups and Pandari bhajana singers also use the maddela with harmonium, cymbals and other instruments.18,19,20

Bashipangu Venkanna writes that Chindu Bhagotam is disappearing in Telangana for lack of training and calls for a government academy for the art.11
