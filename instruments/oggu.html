ఒగ్గు

Oggu

Also known as Jaggu (జగ్గు), Jeggu (జెగ్గు), Damarukam (the general name for this kind of drum) (ఢమరుకం)

A small double-headed hourglass drum - the Telangana name for the damaru held by Shiva - which gives the Oggu Katha ballad tradition of the Kuruma and Golla communities its name. It is sounded at the start of the story and in many scenes of the narration.

About

Oggu (ఒగ్గు) is the name a section of the Shaiva tradition in Telangana gives to the damaru, the small hourglass drum held by Shiva; it is also called jeggu or jaggu.1,3 Telugu Wikipedia describes oggu as a purely native word heard only in the Telangana region.3 Because storytellers use this drum while narrating, their art is called Oggu Katha, 'the story told with the oggu'.1,4,7

The oggu belongs to the Kurumas and Gollas (Yadavas), pastoral communities whose caste deities Mallanna (Mallikarjuna) and Beerappa are the heroes of the stories.1,5 The Kurumas who tell these stories are also called oggollu or baggu vallu, and they serve as priests within their own community.1

How it is made

Sources describe the oggu as a skin-headed drum resembling the damaru in Shiva's hand.6,9 According to Telugu Wikipedia it is about a hand-span (janedu) long, with a middle section of brass or wood and goat skin stretched tight over both ends.3

How it is played

Before a story begins, the performers play the dolu, sound a veeranam and rattle the damaru ('dada dada'), and their first prayer is to the goddess Ganga.1 During the narration the oggu is played in many scenes to keep time and mark events, and it can be treated as a character in the story - for example, it is cradled like a baby when a lullaby scene comes. In the flow of the story it is a little less prominent than the big dolu drum.3

An Oggu Katha troupe usually has five to ten members: a lead narrator and an assistant in front, and behind them the players of the dolu, the talam (cymbals) and the kanjira, all of whom also sing the refrains.1 V6 Velugu lists a large dolu, cymbals and the oggu as the main instruments of the form.6

A 2025 study notes a difference in instruments between performers: priests who serve at the big Mallanna temples such as Ainavolu, Komuravelli, Odela and Srisailam use the jaggu, an instrument like Shiva's damarukam, and are therefore called jaggulu, while other performers use the oggu dolu and cymbals.5

Mikkilineni also records Yadava storytellers in Telangana who dress as women, tie on ankle bells and dance with an oggu in hand, accompanied by a large dolu, big cymbals and chorus singers.2

Beliefs & practices

According to the veteran performer Chukka Sattaiah, as recorded by Mikkilineni, the name has this origin: Veerabhadra and Mallikarjuna carried the damaru, called jaggu, and two hundred years ago Sattaiah's forefathers, grazing the sheep that Mallikarjuna had given them, told these stories under the trees in the forest to the sound of the jaggu. Over time the 'baggu katha told with the jaggu' came to be called oggu katha.1

For the Kurumas, Oggu Katha is a religious duty as well as an art. The Mallanna story is told at Kuruma weddings, and the marriage is performed only after it has been told. Each priest-storyteller once had fixed village and taluk boundaries: he could not conduct weddings in another's area or tell the stories at other times, on pain of expulsion from the caste. Chukka Sattaiah himself is said to have been expelled before he turned Oggu Katha into a popular stage art that the caste gurus later followed.1 A copper-plate record concerning Birla Buchhaiah of Atmakur (Nalgonda district) documents the hereditary (mirasi) rights of the Oggus to perform Beerappa festivals.5

Telugu Wikipedia describes an initiation called oggu diksha. With the permission of caste elders, a trainee goes to a Shaiva shrine, spreads a mat in the temple courtyard, wears sacred ash and meditates on Mallanna; senior oggus then teach him a mantra and bless him, and he wears a necklace of seven cowrie shells said to be given by Yellamma. Only after this is he entitled to tell the stories of Beeranna and Mallanna.3 English Wikipedia links the seven shells to a legend in which Mallanna turned the seven brothers of his bride into dogs and, when she pleaded for them, allowed them to become oggus who narrate his story.4,5

Oggu Katha opens with praise of the goddess Ujjaini Mahankali, followed by prayers to Ganapati and Sarada; V6 Velugu notes this as unusual, since most Hindu performances begin with Ganapati.1,6

Where you will hear it

The oggu is used in Oggu Katha, the sung, danced and acted narration of the lives of Mallanna, Beerappa, Yellamma, Nalla Pochamma, Mandhata and other figures.4,5,6 Performances take place at Kuruma and Golla weddings, at Beerappa festivals and at Mallanna temples and jataras.1,4,5

The form belongs to Telangana. In 1992 Mikkilineni found it mainly in Warangal, Nalgonda and Hyderabad districts, with about fifty troupes; later reports add Karimnagar and Rangareddy, and V6 Velugu notes that the Vemulawada area has many troupes.1,8,6

Well-known Oggu Katha artists include Chukka Sattaiah of Janagama, who received a Sangeet Natak Akademi award and an honorary doctorate from Potti Sreeramulu Telugu University, and Midde Ramulu of Karimnagar district; Jamma Mallari is known as the first woman to perform Oggu Katha.8,4,5 In 2022 The Hans India reported that about 300 artistes depended on the art and that a troupe earned around Rs 5,000 per performance, with only a couple of bookings a month.8

Research notes: points where sources differ or could not be confirmed
  • The word oggu is used loosely: it names the small hourglass drum, but the big barrel drum of the same tradition is called oggu dolu (also Veerappa dolu), and English sources often mix up the Oggu Katha instruments (English Wikipedia names a big drum 'rana bheri' and a horn 'napheera'). This page covers only the small hourglass drum. [1][4]
  • The physical description (hand-span long, brass or wooden middle, goat skin on both sides) comes only from a section of Telugu Wikipedia without an inline citation and was not confirmed elsewhere. No source describes whether the oggu has knotted striker cords like a typical damaru, how its heads are fixed, or who makes it; Mikkilineni's phrase 'rattling the damaru' suggests it is shaken. [3][1]
  • C. P. Brown's dictionary (1903) gives oggu only as a verb ('to yield, to bend, to spread') with no drum meaning, which fits Telugu Wikipedia's statement that the drum sense is a regional Telangana word. [10][3]
  • The oggu diksha and the seven-shell legend are taken from Wikipedia articles without inline citations; the seven brothers turned into dogs also appear in Sarma (2025), but the link to the shell necklace was not confirmed. [3][4][5]
  • Sarma (2025) cites N. Jnaneswara Bhikshu's PhD thesis (Shodhganga) for the jaggu/jaggulu distinction; the thesis itself could not be opened. [5]
  • In Rayalaseema (Kurnool district) the Goravayyas also play a damaru in Mallanna worship, and Telugu Wikipedia says some there are called oggappalu because of a ritual called oggu seva. Whether this is connected with the Telangana oggu was not established.
  • Mikkilineni's chapter gives two different openings: in one place the performers first pray to Ganga after sounding the dolu and damaru, in another the story opens with praise of Ujjaini Mahankali. Practice may differ between troupes. [1][6]
  • Mikkilineni reports that Chukka Sattaiah's storytelling group called its stories 'Veerappa katha', about Veerabhadra, whereas other sources treat Beerappa as a separate Kuruma deity; the relation between the names was not resolved. [1][5]
