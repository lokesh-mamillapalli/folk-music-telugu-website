Parra

Also known as Para (పారా)

A small double-headed wooden drum of the Raj Gonds of Adilabad, played with the fingers of both hands. It is one of the sacred Dandari drums, worshipped at the start of the festival, and gives its name to a lively Dandari dance.

About

The parra (also written para) is a small two-headed drum used by the Raj Gonds of the Adilabad region of northern Telangana, mainly in the Dandari festival season before Diwali1,2. Together with the gumela and vette drums and the kodal dance sticks it belongs to the set of Dandari instruments that are worshipped at the start of the festival3,5.

It appears in a list of eighteen Gond instruments that, according to a 2013 account from Adilabad district, children learn at a traditional Gond learning centre called Gotul Pito6. The English Wikipedia describes the parra as a rhythm instrument used to keep the beat in the Gussadi dance5.

How it is made

Haimendorf described para as small double-membrane drums that thicken slightly in the middle1. The Telugu writer Mikkilineni Radhakrishna Murthy compares its shape with the maddela drum, gives its length as about two feet, and says both ends are covered with goat skin2.

According to Haimendorf, a new para is carved from the wood of a kurm tree or, if none can be found, a vengun tree, because only the fine-grained wood of these two trees is considered suitable. The tree must have a natural hollow running up from its base, and the section containing this hollow becomes the drum shell. After felling, a piece of the trunk is carried to the village, where any experienced craftsman carves the shell; membranes of shaved and dried goat hide, or the skin of any wild animal, are stretched over both ends and held taut with criss-cross thongs of cow and goat hide1.

How it is played

The para is played with the fingers of both hands. The player sits cross-legged and rests the drum against one knee1,2. Haimendorf described the 'small handy para, beaten caressingly with gentle fingers' at night-time dances in the rainy season1.

In the Dandari ensemble each para goes with one or two vette (turbuli) drums1. Haimendorf heard 'the sharp rapid clicking of the turbuli and the deep tone of a para' give a quick, vivacious rhythm for a figure-of-eight dance of young men that was performed without singing1.

Beliefs & practices

Making a new para is a ritual act. Haimendorf recorded that, after the Akadi rite and before the Dandari season, a man from a family with the traditional right to keep a para goes to the forest with friends, a chicken and food offerings. He waves incense before the chosen tree and prays to the dance gods Dundria Raur and Sipiserma Raur for luck and the well-being of his children, marks the trunk with red powder (a line and as many dots as his clan has 'brothers'), and sacrifices the chicken so that its blood marks the bark. He offers the roasted liver and millet bread, throws grain into the branches and embraces the tree, first with the right and then with the left shoulder; the two men who will fell it do the same, and with each of the first six or seven axe strokes a wish is spoken, such as for good health, good harvests or more cattle1.

Before a new para is played for the first time, a chicken is sacrificed in honour of Dundria Raur, patron of dance and song. The drums are sprinkled with water, vermilion and turmeric, a little vermilion powder is dropped into the small hole in the shell of a new para, and those present pray to the dance gods that the drums may sound well and that no harm may befall the dancers1.

Ownership was inherited. Haimendorf wrote that anyone may play the dance drums but only men with a hereditary right could own a para or gumela, and that he knew of no man who owned both1. In a Dandari legend recited by Pardhan bards, the ancestors of the Raur clans resolve to make golden para drums and then take their para drums to the grandmother goddess Padmalpuri to dance the dance of greeting1.

Today, on Bhogi, the first day of the Dandari festival, the parra is worshipped together with the gummela, vette and kodal before the Gussadi costumes are put on3,5. At the end of the festival, drums such as the gumela, parra and vette are put away until the next Akadi festival4.

Where you will hear it

The para accompanies Dandari dancing. Haimendorf wrote that one lively type of dance, more energetic than the dances to melodious gumela songs, 'is called para after the drum used in accompaniment', and his photographs include a boys' para dance1.

Para drums are also played outside the festival itself: Haimendorf described boys gathering round fires at night with para and gumela drums before the first-fruits rite (Nowon), singing songs about the vegetables they could not yet eat1.

The Dandari–Gussadi festival during which the parra is played is celebrated by Raj Gonds and Kolams in Adilabad, Kumuram Bheem Asifabad, Nirmal and Mancherial districts of Telangana and in neighbouring parts of Maharashtra4.

Research notes: points where sources differ or could not be confirmed
  • Spelling: para (Haimendorf, Sphoorthi Theatre), parra (English Wikipedia), పారా (Mikkilineni) and పర్ర (Telugu Wikipedia).
  • Size: Haimendorf calls the para 'small' and 'handy', while Mikkilineni gives a length of about two feet; no measured examples were found.
  • The tree names 'kurm' and 'vengun' are Haimendorf's Gondi forms; their botanical identities were not confirmed (the OCR text of his book is partly damaged at this point).
  • The detailed making and consecration rituals come from Haimendorf's fieldwork in the 1940s; whether they are still followed was not confirmed by a recent source.
  • The English Wikipedia description of the parra as 'a rhythm instrument used to keep the beat' has no citation.
  • No free image was checked for this instrument (media research was dropped from scope); some online photographs of Dandari instrument worship show small laced double-headed drums that may be parras, but they are not labelled.
