పెప్రే

Pepre

Also known as Pepri (పెప్రె), Pipri (పిప్రి), Pipre (పిప్రే), Pepera, Sanayi (in a Gond instrument list) (సన్నాయి)

A shawm of the sannayi type played by the Pardhan musicians of the Raj Gonds in Adilabad. Played in pairs with the kikri fiddle and drums, it sounds at Gond clan-god rites, in the Dandari–Gussadi festival and at the Nagoba Jatara.

About

The pepre (also written pepri, pipri, pipre or pepera) is a wind instrument used in the ritual and festive music of the Raj Gonds of the Adilabad region of northern Telangana3,4,5. A Telugu account based on the book Mooladhwani describes the pepre as sannayi (shawm) instruments, two of which are played together with the kikri fiddle and a skin drum called dakki2.

In his 1948 study of the Adilabad Gonds, Haimendorf illustrated the pepre and the bonga as 'Pardhan trumpets', noting that the two are usually played together, and he photographed Kanaka Ramu, a Pardhan of Pulera village, playing the pepre1.

How it is made

Telugu sources place the pepre in the sannayi family2, and the English Wikipedia describes it simply as a wind instrument that sounds like a flute or trumpet4. No detailed published account of its materials, making or makers was found during this research2,4.

How it is played

The pepre is played in pairs. The Telugu account speaks of two pepre played together with the kikri and the dakki drum, and in Gond epic songs recorded by Haimendorf the phrase 'jora peprena' (a pair of pepre) is used when trumpets sound at a wedding1,2.

Haimendorf wrote that at festivals the most prominent Pardhan usually plays the kingri fiddle while younger men blow trumpets and beat drums1. A 2013 list of eighteen Gond instruments from Adilabad district notes that the sanai, kikiri and kalikom are the instruments of the Pardhans7.

Beliefs & practices

A Gond origin myth recorded by Haimendorf explains why the Pardhans play wind instruments. When the culture hero Pahandi Kupar Lingal first organised the worship of the Gond gods, he asked the Gonds to play the fiddle, the trumpet and the drums. They refused to play fiddle and trumpet, saying that they would then be made to sit apart at feasts, so Lingal made a fiddle and a trumpet himself and the gods appointed Hirasuka as the first Pardhan to serve in the cult; then 'the Pardhan played fiddle and trumpet, and the Gonds beat the drums'1.

Haimendorf also recorded another version, in which Hirasuka played only the fiddle and the Pardhans learnt to blow trumpets much later from musicians of the Mang caste1.

Mikkilineni Radhakrishna Murthy wrote that instruments such as the dappu, tudumu and pipre give energy to the Gusadi dance troupe, and that the Gonds regard the musical instruments and Gusadi dance equipment as highly sacred and worship them before the dance begins8.

Where you will hear it

Pardhan trumpets are heard at important moments of Gond religious rites. Haimendorf described 'the wail of Pardhan trumpets' breaking the stillness at first cock-crow on the day of a clan-god (Persa Pen) feast, trumpets and drums sounding as sacrifices were made, and Pardhans blowing trumpets beside the drummers at other village rites, where their role was purely that of musicians1.

The pepre is among the instruments of the Dandari–Gussadi festival held around Diwali. It is listed with the dappu, tudumu and kalikom in Telugu and English accounts of the festival, and Village Square names the pepera among the instruments used in Gussadi celebrations3,4,6,8.

At the Nagoba Jatara of the Mesram clan of Gonds at Keslapur, Indervelli mandal, the Deccan Chronicle reported in 2025 that the Adivasi instruments kalikom, dhol, thudum and pepri are played differently to suit each ritual or occasion5.
