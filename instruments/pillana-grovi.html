Pillana Grovi

Also known as Venuvu (వేణువు), Murali (మురళి), Pillana govi (పిల్లన గోవి), Pillangovi / Pillangrovi (పిల్లంగోవి / పిల్లంగ్రోవి), Veduru pillana grovi (bamboo flute) (వెదురు పిల్లన గ్రోవి)

The pillana grovi is the Telugu name for the simple bamboo flute, the instrument of Krishna. Village flutes of this kind were widely played in the Godavari region, and Telugu ballads describe herders playing them to call their calves.

About

Pillana grovi (also venuvu or murali) is the Telugu name for the bamboo flute. Telugu Wikipedia describes the venuvu, murali or pillanagrovi as a wind instrument made of well-dried bamboo, and the English Wikipedia article on the South Indian venu gives pillana grovi as its Telugu name. In 1891 C. R. Day recorded the bamboo flute of Southern India and the Deccan as the 'Pillagovi, or Murali'.1,2,3

The same simple instrument appears in village music and in classical music. The flute used in Carnatic concerts took its present form through 20th-century changes associated with Sarabha Sastri and T. R. Mahalingam. For the villages, Mikkilineni Radhakrishna Murthy's survey of Telugu folk arts describes a short bamboo pillana grovi that was very widely played in the villages of the Godavari region and was loved by village people elsewhere as well.1,2,4

How it is made

The flute is made from well-seasoned bamboo. The tube is closed at one end and open at the other, with a blowing hole near the top. Below it are finger holes, which Telugu Wikipedia says number three in some flutes and eight in others. The quality of the sound depends partly on the bamboo used.1

The village flute described by Mikkilineni has one blowing hole and five small finger holes running from the middle to the far end, and is usually about 21 centimetres long. The classical venu is larger, with a blowing hole and eight closely placed finger holes, and is made in various sizes.4,2

How it is played

The player holds the flute sideways and blows across the blowing hole, using the fingers of both hands to close and open the finger holes. By overblowing and cross-fingering the classical flute reaches about two and a half octaves, and sliding the fingers on and off the holes produces the ornaments (gamakas) of Indian music.2

In the same survey, Mikkilineni describes another flute 'in use in many places in the Telugu country'. It is blown through a hole at one end and fingered with both hands, and is customarily played at weddings and other auspicious occasions and in leisure time.4

Beliefs & practices

The flute is Krishna's instrument. C. R. Day noted that the pillagovi was traditionally believed to have been invented by Krishna, who is usually shown holding or playing it, and that the flute was held to be especially sacred. Telugu Wikipedia notes that Krishna is traditionally called venugana lola, 'the one who delights in the music of the flute'.3,1

The flute also belongs to the herding world of Telugu ballads. The Katamaraju Kathalu, the heroic ballad cycle of the Yadava (Golla) herders, describe the great herds setting out, 'playing pillanagrovis and calling the calves'. They also describe processions that move to tappeta drums, pillangrovis and horns. The texts were edited by Tangirala Venkata Subba Rao, largely from palm-leaf manuscripts kept by the traditional singers.6

Where you will hear it

Besides everyday village playing in the Godavari region, Mikkilineni writes that in the Kakatiya period people blew pillana grovis while playing games and dancing the circular gondli dance during the Bathukamma and Boddemma festivals.4,5

In the Katamaraju ballads flutes are heard with drums and horns in the herders' processions. The same bamboo flute is also played in Carnatic music, where the venu took its present concert form in the 20th century.6,2

Research notes: points where sources differ or could not be confirmed
  • The origin of the name 'pillana grovi' was not found in the sources consulted.
  • Mikkilineni (1992, p. 387) quotes a Krishna bhajana in which the child Krishna is dressed with ankle bells, rings and a peacock feather and given a 'పిల్లంగోరు' in his hand. The context suggests the flute, but both the scan and the Wikisource transcription read 'పిల్లంగోరు', not 'పిల్లంగోవి', so it was not used as evidence.
  • Finger-hole counts vary between sources: 3 or 8 (Telugu Wikipedia), 5 (Mikkilineni's village flute), 8 (Carnatic venu).
  • Mikkilineni's second flute, 'in use in many places in the Telugu country', is listed in his tribal-instruments section as a second kind of 'panar' and is said to be blown 'from a hole at one end'. It is unclear whether it is side-blown like the pillana grovi or end-blown.
  • The Kakatiya-period statement about flutes in the gondli dance at Bathukamma and Boddemma is Mikkilineni's summary; the passage does not name its original medieval source.
  • No source consulted names present-day flute makers or village flute players in Andhra Pradesh or Telangana, so the current state of folk flute playing could not be confirmed.
  • C. R. Day says the end-blown 'nuy' flute, not the side-blown pillagovi, was 'much used by shepherds and cowherds'; the herders' flutes in the Katamaraju ballads are simply called pillanagrovi, and their exact form is not described.
  • Tribal flutes (for example the Savara 'panar' and the flute called 'pirodi' used in peacock dances) are left to the tribal-instruments research.
