Sannayi (Nadaswaram)

Also known as Nadaswaram (నాదస్వరం), Nagaswaram (నాగస్వరం), Nagasaram (నాగసరము), Sannayi melam (the sannayi-and-dolu ensemble) (సన్నాయి మేళం), Mangala vadyam (auspicious instrument) (మంగళ వాద్యం)

Sannayi is the everyday Telugu name for the nadaswaram, the long double-reed pipe whose sound is considered auspicious. Played with the dolu drum, it accompanies weddings, temple rituals, village festivals and the Sankranti rounds of the Gangireddu bull performers.

About

In Telugu, the long double-reed pipe known in Carnatic music as the nadaswaram is usually called the sannayi. Telugu Wikipedia states that Telugu people call the nadaswaram 'sannayi', and C. P. Brown's Telugu–English dictionary of 1903 defines సన్నాయి as a kind of clarionet and gives నాగసరము (nagasaram) as its equivalent. J. P. L. Gwynn's modern dictionary glosses sannayi as a kind of clarinet and nadaswaram as an instrument similar to an oboe. Because these sources treat the two words as names for one instrument, this archive describes them on a single page.1,2,3

The nadaswaram is a double-reed instrument with a conical bore that widens towards the lower end, where it ends in a bell. It is among the loudest non-brass acoustic instruments and is best suited to open-air playing. In South Indian culture it is considered very auspicious: it belongs to the family of mangala vadyam ('auspicious instruments') and is heard at almost all Hindu weddings and in temples, including in Andhra Pradesh and Telangana.4,5

Writing in 1891 about the music of Southern India and the Deccan, C. R. Day quoted Colonel Meadows Taylor's description of these pipes as the only regular outdoor instruments of Indian music. They were used at domestic and public religious ceremonies, processions, festivals and temple music, with different music for marriages, rejoicings, funerals, welcomings and departures.6

How it is made

C. R. Day described the nagasara as a reed pipe with a conical bore, usually pierced with twelve holes. Only the upper seven are fingered; the others are stopped with wax as the player chooses, to set the pitch. The reed, broad like a bassoon reed, is mounted on a short metal staple. The body was usually of a dark, close-grained wood with a metal bell, and spare reeds and an ivory pin for adjusting them hung from the pipe.6

Modern descriptions give the traditional body wood as aacha, although bamboo, sandalwood, copper, brass, ebony and ivory are also used. Old, seasoned wood is considered best, sometimes salvaged from demolished old houses. The reed (seevali) is made from a dried grass and sits in a small metal cylinder on a staple at the top. The pipe has seven finger holes plus five extra holes near the bottom that can be stopped with wax to change the tone, and a range of about two and a half octaves. Kamala Ganesh describes an instrument with an ebony body and a rosewood bell.4,5

The best-documented place where nadaswarams are made is Narasingampettai near Thanjavur in Tamil Nadu. There a few families turn the wood on lathes, drill the seven finger holes, smooth the body and fit the reed mouthpiece and the bell. The sources used for this page do not describe instrument makers in Andhra Pradesh or Telangana.7

How it is played

The reed at the top of the pipe is held between the player's lips. Fingering, breathing and tonguing all need great stamina and skill. Semitones and finer shades are produced by changing the pressure of the breath, and by only partly covering the holes.4,5,6

The sannayi is usually played in pairs with drums: the thavil, which Telugu Wikipedia equates with the dolu. A drone pipe (ottu or sruti) is tuned to the keynote. Telugu sources call the nadaswaram and dolu together the mangala vadyams, and the wedding band built around them is called a sannayi melam.4,6,1

In folk processions the sannayi plays fast, exciting music called veerangam. At Veerabhadra festivals and large jataras, Veerashaiva devotees perform a sword dance in front of decorated prabhas, with two or three groups of sannayi players walking ahead. The dolu drums beat very fast while the sannayis are blown at the highest pitch close to the sword-bearer's ears, rousing him to start the Daksha Yajna dandakam.8

Beliefs & practices

Among Telugu Hindus the sannayi is tied to the barber-musician community now called Nayi Brahmin (listed with the names Mangali, Mangala and Bhajantri). Telugu Wikipedia notes that the community has long been known for its dolu and nadaswaram players, and links the name Mangala to the mangala vadyams they played. Edgar Thurston's survey of 1909 records that the Telugu barber caste Mangala took part in marriages 'by the melodious sounds of their flutes (nagasaram)'. It also records that at auspicious events a barber was called Bajantri, meaning musician, and that in the Vizagapatam district he was the village musician.9,10,11

A second hereditary tradition belongs to the Dudekula, a Telugu-speaking Muslim community. Sociologist Kamala Ganesh describes families of Muslim nadaswaram players in Guntur, Nellore, Vijayawada, Ongole and other places in Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, many attached by hereditary right to local Hindu temples. A 1986 Telugu compendium by Bhusurapalle Venkateswarlu lists 107 Muslim players among 195 nadaswaram artists from the early 19th century onwards. It also names teaching centres (gurukulams) at Chilakaluripet, Guntur, Bandar, Kommuru, Rajahmundry and Kumkalagunta, supported by local zamindars and temples.5,12

Temple service carried hereditary rights. The family of Sheik Chinna Moulana of Karavadi in Prakasam district held the right to play at the village Rama temple through a land grant (manyam) from a local zamindar for about three centuries. A family member still plays for the deity there.5,12

The sannayi is also part of shared village festivals. Mikkilineni Radhakrishna Murthy describes Pirla Panduga (Muharram) in Telugu villages: the pirs are carried through the village with a sannayi band and songs of mourning, and on the last night devotees carrying the pirs run through the fire pit amid loud sannayi and dappu music.13

Where you will hear it

Weddings and temples remain the main settings. Among notable temple musicians, Gosaveedu Shaik Hassan (1928–2021) of Krishna district served as resident musician (asthana vidwan) of the Sita Ramachandraswamy temple at Bhadrachalam from 1950 to 1993. The Kasim–Babu brothers, grandsons of Sheik Chinna Moulana, have served as special nadaswaram artists of the Tirumala Tirupati Devasthanams.14,15

During the Sankranti season the Gangireddula performers go from house to house with a decorated bull. Their gear is described as a drone (sruti) and sannayi pipe, a dolu and a small bronze bell, and they play dolu and sannayi while singing folk songs and asking for alms. The bull is trained to move and bow to the music, and people consider it lucky when a Gangireddu stops at their door. In Visakhapatnam in 2025 the community was reported playing nadaswaram and drums. In 2026 V6 Velugu reported that fewer Gangireddu performers now come out in cities and villages alike: once seen all through January, they now appear only here and there, even on the three festival days.16,17,18,19,20

At Shaiva festivals and village jataras the sannayi leads the music. B. Ramaraju describes the Veeranatyam, danced by devotees at the Lingodbhava hour on Shivaratri, accompanied by the veeranam drum, dolu, tasha-marfa and sannayis. Mikkilineni describes goddess jataras in Krishna and Guntur districts, held after epidemics, that were celebrated with the veerangam of sannayi players and tappeta drums. At the yearly festival of the Palnadu heroes at Karempudi, he saw Perini dance movements performed before the heroes' images, driven by the veerangam of sannayis.18,13,21

Andhra Pradesh has produced leading concert players. Sheik Chinna Moulana (1924–1999) of Karavadi received the Padma Shri and the Madras Music Academy's Sangeetha Kalanidhi. The Andhra-born Sheik Mahaboob Subhani and his wife Kaleeshabi Mahaboob received the Padma Shri in 2020, and Kaleeshabi is described as the first Muslim woman to play the nadaswaram in public. Gosaveedu Shaik Hassan was awarded the Padma Shri after his death, in 2022. By the 1980s concert opportunities for the nadaswaram had begun to shrink, and in hereditary Muslim families many younger members now take up other work, although a few in each family still learn the instrument.22,23,5,14,12

Research notes: points where sources differ or could not be confirmed
  • One page or two: Telugu Wikipedia (సన్నాయి redirects to నాదస్వరం), C. P. Brown's 1903 dictionary (సన్నాయి = నాగసరము) and folk-arts books all use 'sannayi' for the nadaswaram-type pipe, so one page was made. C. R. Day (1891) also describes a smaller relative, the mukhavina, about half the size. The pipes carried by itinerant folk troupes may therefore be shorter than concert nadaswarams, but no source consulted describes a separate Telugu 'sannayi' distinct from the nadaswaram.
  • The sources disagree on materials and holes: Wikipedia gives aacha wood with 7 finger holes plus 5 wax-stopped holes; Day gives twelve holes (seven fingered) and a dark close-grained wood; Kamala Ganesh gives an ebony body with a rosewood bell.
  • No source consulted describes nadaswaram or reed makers in Andhra Pradesh or Telangana; the making process cited is from Narasingampettai, Tamil Nadu.
  • Telugu Wikipedia's articles on నాదస్వరం and నాయీ బ్రాహ్మణులు contain unsourced historical claims about the community's ancient origins; only the widely supported link between the community and dolu/nadaswaram playing is used here. The explanation of 'mangala vadyam' as 'instruments played by the Mangala people' is a folk etymology; Thurston links the name Mangala to 'mangalam' (auspiciousness).
  • Sheik Mahaboob Subhani's origin: Wikipedia says he was born in Prakasam district, while Kamala Ganesh (NCPA) says the family is originally from Satuluru in Guntur district.
  • Gangireddu troupes: the Deccan Chronicle (2025) says 'nadaswaram and drums', while Telugu sources say sannayi and dolu. This fits the two names referring to one instrument, but the exact pipes played by these troupes were not examined.
  • The Pirla Panduga, goddess-jatara, Veerabhadra and Perini descriptions come from Mikkilineni's 1992 book and may not reflect present-day practice. Thurston's 1909 survey uses colonial-era language and is used only for occupational facts.
