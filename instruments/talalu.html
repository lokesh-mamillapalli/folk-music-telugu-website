Talalu

Also known as Talam (singular) (తాళం), Chetalalu (hand cymbals) (చేతాళాలు), Karatalalu (కరతాళాలు), Manjira / jalra / gini (మంజీర / జల్రా / గిని), Pedda talalu (large cymbals) (పెద్ద తాళాలు)

Talalu are small cymbals of bronze or other bell metal, joined by a cord and clashed together to mark the beat. They keep time in village bhajanas and in Telangana's Oggu Katha and Chindu Bhagavatam, and larger talalu ring out in forms such as the Kola Sambaram of the Godavari districts.

About

Talalu (singular talam) are a pair of metal cymbals struck against each other to keep rhythm. Telugu Wikipedia describes them as two bronze discs, also called manjira, jalra or gini, notes that the word is connected with clapping, and says that in Hindu devotional use they are also called karatalalu, 'hand cymbals'1. C. P. Brown's Telugu-English Dictionary (1903) gives tāḷamu both as 'time or measure in music' and as 'a cymbal, because used to keep time with', and records tāḷagiri for a cymbal player2.

Because they mark the tala (rhythmic cycle), talalu appear in a very wide range of Telugu devotional and folk ensembles: seated bhajanas, dance bhajanas, Kolatam, and the long narrative performances of Telangana such as Oggu Katha3,4,5.

How it is made

According to Telugu Wikipedia, talalu are made of bell metal, for which the article lists brass, bronze, copper, zinc and similar materials. A cord is tied through the hole in the middle of the cymbals, each cymbal is held together with its cord while playing, and the pitch of the sound depends on the size and weight of the cymbals1. Brown's dictionary likewise mentions the cymbal as a kanchu vadyamu, an instrument of bell metal6.

Sizes vary between traditions. The talalu used in Oggu Katha are described as about twice the size of a palm, and smaller talalu are also used alongside them4. The Kola Sambaram storytellers of the Godavari districts use large talalu that give a loud sound7, and Telangana Yadava storytellers of the Golla Suddulu tradition are described performing with a large drum and large talalu8.

How it is played

The player holds one cymbal in each hand by its cord and strikes the pair together on the beats1. In an Oggu Katha troupe, which needs at least five members, the main narrator and his assistant stand in front and three musicians behind them play the dolu drum, the talam and the kanjira, also singing the chorus; the talam player takes up the kanjira when needed4. Mikkilineni notes that the talalu and dolu are played from the beginning to the end of an Oggu Katha performance9, and Telugu Wikipedia lists rhythmic patterns used in Oggu Katha such as mendu talam, duyyara, adi talam and mendu bhairavi4.

In the Chindu Bhagavatam troupes of Nizamabad district, the actors sing their own songs and verses while women of the troupe play the talalu; mridangam, harmonium and ankle bells are also used10.

In the Kola Sambaram, a night-long storytelling performance about Lord Venkateswara, two men at the front swing burning torches (kolalu) while two singers at the back play large talalu and sing the refrain for the narrator7. In Adilabad district, Kolatam songs sung by elders are accompanied by harmonium and talalu5.

Beliefs & practices

Singing with talalu is a communal act of devotion. Mikkilineni describes villagers singing bhajanas together while sounding talalu in rhythm; energetic young men perform 'savamulla' bhajanas, which he explains as talala bhajanas (cymbal bhajanas) that involve dancing, while older men sit and sing Hari and Pandari bhajanas with hand cymbals and chirutalu. At Sri Rama Navami and Dasara, bhajana groups also stage plays in bhajana style through the night3.

Oggu Katha, in which talalu are central, is a hereditary storytelling tradition of the Kuruma and Golla communities, who tell the stories of Beerappa and Mallanna. Telugu Wikipedia describes an 'Oggu deeksha' initiation: with the permission of caste elders, a trainee goes to a Shaiva shrine, meditates on Mallanna, receives a mantra and blessing from the Oggu elders and wears a necklace of seven cowries associated with Yellamma before becoming an Oggu performer4. Performances begin with praise of Ujjaini Mahankali followed by prayers to Ganapati and Sarada9.

The Kola Sambaram with its large talalu is itself a devotional event: it is held at night on the day of deeparadhana by those who have returned from a pilgrimage to Tirupati, and tells a folk version of the love story of Venkateswara, Alamelu Manga and Bibi Nanchari; one of the performers becomes possessed during the performance, and in the past a bull was brought and set free afterwards7.

Where you will hear it

Talalu are used in Oggu Katha across Telangana4,11, in Chindu Bhagavatam (Mikkilineni counted about fifty troupes in Nizamabad district)10, and in the street plays of the Telaga Dasaris or Gante Bhagavatulu of Nalgonda, Khammam, Warangal and Hyderabad districts, where actors sing to harmonium, maddela and talalu12. Budige Jangams performing daytime pagati veshalu (disguise performances) go from house to house with harmonium, tabla and talalu13.

In Karimnagar district, street plays (veedhi bhagotham) are accompanied by maddela, talam and harmonium14. Talalu also accompany Pandari bhajanas15, Kolatam in Adilabad5, and the Kola Sambaram, which is performed mostly in the East and West Godavari districts7.

Research notes: points where sources differ or could not be confirmed
  • The Telugu word talam also means the rhythmic cycle (tala), so some mentions of 'talam' in sources may refer to rhythm rather than the instrument.
  • The list of metals comes from Telugu Wikipedia's general article; no source describing the alloy or the makers of the talalu used by folk troupes was found.
  • Mikkilineni's instrument list for Chindu Bhagavatam also includes 'chippalu', which could not be identified.
  • No beliefs, taboos or making rituals specific to talalu were found; the rituals described relate to the art forms in which they are played.
  • The fifty Chindu Bhagavatam troupes and other details from Mikkilineni's book date from 1992.
