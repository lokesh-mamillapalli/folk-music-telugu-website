తప్పెట

Tappeta

Also known as Thappeta (తప్పెట), Tappeta gullu (the dance and its drums) (తప్పెట గుళ్ళు), Tappeta gunde (the chest drum) (తప్పెటగుండె)

A small, flat, round drum tied to the chest and beaten with both hands by the dancers of Tappeta Gullu, the devotional dance of the Golla (Yadava) herding community of Srikakulam, Vizianagaram and Visakhapatnam in North Coastal Andhra. In everyday Telugu the word tappeta is also used for the dappu.

About

Tappeta (తప్పెట) is an old Telugu word for a drum. C. P. Brown's Telugu-English Dictionary (1903) defines both tappeta and dappu as 'a large drum', the Sabdaratnakaram (1912) explains dappu simply as 'tappeta', and G. N. Reddy's Telugu dictionary (1979) calls the dappu 'the tappeta used for making public announcements'.1,2,3

The two words are used for the same frame drum in different regions. The Telugu Akademi's regional dialect dictionary (2004) records tappeta and palaka as the Rayalaseema words for the dappu, and the Andhra Pradesh Sahitya Akademi's dialect dictionary (1970) records tappeta in the sense of dappu from Nellore, Kurnool, Telangana and Adilabad. The writer on folk arts Mikkilineni Radhakrishna Murthy likewise writes that the dappu dance is also called tappetla vayidyam, and kanaka tappetlu in Rayalaseema, and that it is mostly played by the Madiga community.1,2,4

In North Coastal Andhra, however, the name is best known through Tappeta Gullu (తప్పెట గుళ్ళు), a dance in which every performer wears his own small drum on his chest, described by the Andhra Pradesh Shilparamam society as 'the Tappeta or gundlu, which is hung on the chest'. Mikkilineni describes it as a tappeta-like instrument made round out of metal sheet, tied on the chest and played vigorously with both hands. This page is about that chest drum.5,6,7

The word itself is Dravidian: the Dravidian Etymological Dictionary groups Telugu tappeṭa with Tamil tappaṭṭai and Kannada tappaṭe and tamaṭe, names for drums and tambourines. The Hans India explains the second half of the dance's name, gullu, as 'alarm', and repeats the local account that the drumming was once used by cowherds to keep wild animals away from grazing cattle.8,9

How it is made

Detailed descriptions of how the Tappeta Gullu drum is made are scarce. Mikkilineni's survey of Telugu folk arts, published by Telugu University in 1992, describes it as an instrument made round out of metal sheet (rēku), and the Telugu Wikipedia article on the dance repeats that the tappeta gunde is made round from metal sheet and tied over the chest.6,7

Other descriptions call it a flat drum, and Telugu dictionaries classify the tappeta in general as a charma vadyam, an instrument sounded on a stretched skin.10,1

How it is played

The drum is tied so that it hangs on the chest, leaving both hands free. Dancers beat it with both hands in changing rhythmic patterns (gatulu) while wearing ankle bells, moving in a circle, bending, rising and leaping, with shouts and long sung phrases.6,10,11

A troupe has a leader who is also its guru, and the whole performance follows his directions. Sources give troupe sizes of about 12 to 20 men, although a 2024 report describes a troupe of 30 to 35 artistes.6,10,5,9

As a performance builds up, the troupe splits into two groups that try to outdo each other with drum patterns and gamakas. At its height the dancers perform feats while still drumming: climbing onto one another to form a tower, two drummers hanging on either side of a third, or one man standing on the rim of a water-filled clay pot while another, standing on him, keeps drumming and dancing with a full pot of water on his head without spilling it.6

Besides leading the dancing, the leader narrates stories such as the Ramayana, the Mahabharata and the Bobbili war and sings kirtanas in between. Mikkilineni writes that a performance can last four to five hours and is given at night.6

Beliefs & practices

Tappeta Gullu belongs above all to the Golla (Yadava) community, who keep herds of cattle, sheep and goats. Mikkilineni reports, citing Bittu Venkateswarlu, that when the rains fail and even fodder becomes scarce, Tappeta Gullu takes a prominent place in the worship offered to the gods for relief. The Andhra Pradesh Shilparamam society similarly describes the dance as performed by cowherds and shepherds since ancient times to please and propitiate Gangamma, the rain goddess, at village festivals held in her honour, with songs mostly in praise of Vishnu.6,5

The Yadavas worship Gangamma as their clan goddess and believe that Parvati was born as Gangamma, the wife of Katamaraju, at Shiva's command. Their origin story for the instrument says that Shiva gave away musical instruments to all the devotees who came to him, and gave the tappeta gullu that were left over to the Yadavas.6,7

When the dance is performed as a ritual, it begins with a man carrying a decorated pot of water around the village, a symbol of the rain goddess Gangamma.10,11,12

Mikkilineni records several customs around the art. Before performing in a village, a troupe accepts a token advance from the village elders, a quarter-rupee with a little jaggery, and fixes the date. Members work at their occupations by day and perform and practise at night, children are trained from a young age, and the performers are men. The performance is carried out with strict discipline, and any slip in its sequence is considered a disgrace.6,9

Where you will hear it

The dance is practised mainly in Srikakulam, Vizianagaram and Visakhapatnam districts, and Mikkilineni also lists it among the folk arts of East Godavari. It is performed at jataras, at the Gapu sambaralu of the Golla community, at Gangadevi and village-goddess festivals and at Sri Rama koluvu, and is especially associated with Sankranti ('Pedda Panduga').6,13,7,9

Its songs and ballads include the story of Gangamma, the Dasavataralu, Krishna leelalu, Sarangadhara charitra, Chenchita and Ammavari charitra, alongside local songs sung in the North Coastal Andhra dialect.6,7,11

In the early 1990s Mikkilineni found the art alive in Pallapeta and Muripella in Gajapathinagaram taluk of Vizianagaram district, under the leadership of Pellachina Narayana and Durla China Appalaswamy, with about forty villages around Gajapathinagaram having trained troupes, and at Sheru Mahammadpuram near Srikakulam under Kona Chinnavadu and later Korada Potappudu. In 2024 The Hans India profiled the Sri Krishna Tappeta Gullu troupe of Mudara Gollalapalem village (Padmanabham mandal), led by Allaboni Ramu and including third-generation performer Duvvu Shiva. Troupes have performed at Republic Day celebrations in Delhi and abroad, and the dance appears in the Telugu film Rangasthalam.6,9,7

Mikkilineni already described Tappeta Gullu in 1992 as surviving 'with its last breath' in scattered places and waiting for support. In 2024 troupe members told The Hans India that the younger generation had gradually lost interest, and asked the government for more opportunities to perform at temples, carnivals and major events.6,9

Research notes: points where sources differ or could not be confirmed
  • The sources consulted do not describe the playing face of the Tappeta Gullu drum or how it is fixed; Mikkilineni (1992) and Telugu Wikipedia only say the drum is made round from metal sheet. A claim on some websites that it is a cone of tin or wood covered with leather could not be traced to a source that could be read.
  • Name overlap with the dappu: dictionaries treat tappeta and dappu as the same frame drum (tappeta being the Rayalaseema word), while the Tappeta Gullu chest drum is a smaller, different instrument. This page covers the chest drum; the general frame drum belongs on the Dappu page. The Dravidian Etymological Dictionary lists tappeṭa but a search for ḍappu found no entry, so any historical link between the two words is not established here.
  • The explanation of 'gullu' as 'alarm' and the story that the dance began as a way to scare off wild animals come from a newspaper report and look like local explanations; they are not confirmed by scholarly sources.
  • Troupe sizes vary between sources: 12 to 15 (Jai Ho Srikakulam), 12 to 20 (Auchitya), up to about 20 (Mikkilineni) and 30 to 35 (The Hans India, 2024).
  • Telugu Wikipedia (without a cited source) credits a 19th-century Srikakulam poet, Balivada Narayana, with the first Tappeta Gullu ballad, 'Sri Krishna', and names the Korrayi Gangayya and Duryodhana brothers of Gollapeta (Pathapatnam mandal) as keepers of the art. These claims were not confirmed elsewhere.
  • Telugu Traditions and Telugu Wikipedia say the tradition is about 400 years old (Telugu Traditions traces its origin to Srikakulam district); neither gives a source for this figure, so it is not used in the text.
