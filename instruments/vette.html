Vette (Turbuli)

Also known as Turbuli / Turubuli (తురుబులి), Wete, Vetti

A small, shallow drum of the Raj Gonds of Adilabad, made from a clay, iron or wooden bowl covered with skin and struck with two thin bamboo sticks. One of the Dandari festival drums, it gives a sharp, clicking rhythm; according to a Gond legend and a custom recorded in the 1940s, a new vette is touched by a menstruating woman so that it sounds right.

About

The vette (also written wete or vetti) or turbuli is a small single-headed drum with a shallow, saucer-shaped shell. It is used by the Raj Gonds of the Adilabad region of northern Telangana during the Dandari festival season before Diwali1,2,3.

It belongs to the group of Dandari dance drums together with the parra and the gumela: Haimendorf noted that each para, and each pair of gumela, is accompanied by one or two vette drums1. At the start of the festival the vette is worshipped along with the gummela, parra and kodal dance sticks4,6.

How it is made

Haimendorf described turbuli, also known as wete, as saucer-shaped single-membrane drums made of wood, burnt clay or iron, usually not more than ten inches in diameter1. Mikkilineni Radhakrishna Murthy's Telugu account says the Gonds of Adilabad make its plate-like shell from potter's clay, iron or wood and stretch skin over it, but gives the diameter of the skin as usually more than ten inches2.

Although the legend speaks of wooden turbuli, Haimendorf found that Gonds in the 1940s used earthen ones, buying the clay bowl called werni from local potters, or similar iron shells made by a blacksmith1. When buying a wete from a potter the buyer gave a chicken, some grain and often cash, and the goat-skin membrane was attached with iron hoops and leather thongs1.

How it is played

The vette is beaten with two slender splinters of bamboo1. Mikkilineni likewise says it is played with two thin sticks2.

Its sound is sharp and quick. Haimendorf described how 'the sharp rapid clicking of the turbuli and the deep tone of a para' gave a lively rhythm for a Dandari dance of young men performed without songs, and he saw a visiting Dandari troupe arrive with earthen turbuli hung on their shoulders1.

Beliefs & practices

A Dandari legend recited by the Pardhan bards explains a striking custom. The Raur ancestors made para drums, and also turbuli from kursi wood, but the turbuli would not sound true. They asked the wise Pardhan Hirasuka, who said that the Gond gods were unruly and could not be controlled, and advised that the drums be touched by a menstruating woman, and that new shells be bought from the potter and covered with hide. When a menstruating woman touched them, the drums sounded well1.

Haimendorf found the custom still followed in the 1940s: every new wete drum was touched by a menstruating woman, following the advice attributed to Hirasuka. He pointed out that this is remarkable because menstruating women were otherwise thought to defile ritual objects and were kept away from ceremonies, and suggested that the idea may be to neutralise one evil with another1.

As with the other Dandari drums, the first playing of a new wete must be preceded by a chicken sacrifice in honour of Dundria Raur, the patron of dance and song, at which the drums are sprinkled with water, vermilion, turmeric and the sacrificial blood1.

Today, on Bhogi, the first day of Dandari, the vette is worshipped together with the gummela, parra and kodal before the Gussadi costumes are put on, and at the end of the festival drums such as the gumela, parra and vette are put away until the next Akadi festival4,5.

Where you will hear it

The vette is played during the Dandari–Gussadi festival in the Gond villages of Adilabad, Kumuram Bheem Asifabad and parts of Mancherial and Nirmal districts. The Hans India lists the vetti among the Dandari instruments, alongside the dappu, ghumela, dhol, karra and tudum3.

In Haimendorf's account the para and turbuli accompanied the dances of the Dandari troupe, while the dance of the masked Gussadi performers was accompanied instead by the large dapna drums and kettle-drums1.

In 2021 The Hans India reported that these Dandari instruments were almost extinct from the markets3.

Research notes: points where sources differ or could not be confirmed
  • Names: vette (Telugu Wikipedia, Mikkilineni), wete and turbuli (Haimendorf), vetti (The Hans India), turubuli (తురుబులి, Mikkilineni). All appear to refer to the same drum; Haimendorf explicitly says turbuli are also known as wete.
  • Diameter: Haimendorf says usually not more than ten inches, Mikkilineni says usually more than ten inches.
  • In the English translation of the Dandari legend in Haimendorf's book, the Gondi word turburi is rendered 'kettle-drums', although elsewhere he treats turbuli and the iron kettle-drum as different drums.
  • The menstruation custom and the purchase details come from fieldwork in the 1940s; no recent source confirming whether the custom continues was found.
  • 'Karra' in The Hans India list is probably the dance sticks rather than a drum; not confirmed.
